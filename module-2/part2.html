Part 2: What Does “Random Assignment” Actually Require?

What Does Randomization Actually Guarantee?

We tend to talk about randomization as if it were a button. Click “Randomize” in Qualtrics, split people into treatment and control, move on, and act as if the two groups are now basically the same. But what did that button actually promise?

Here is the one-line version of this whole page:

Randomization is a procedure. Exchangeability is what a valid procedure gives you. Balance is what you actually see in the one experiment you ran.

Let’s unpack that in plain terms. Every person has two “what-if” outcomes: what their score would be if they got the treatment, and what it would be if they didn’t. Researchers call these potential outcomes. (We won’t use them properly until “The Fundamental Problem of Causal Inference” (Module 3, Part 1), so for now just think “the what-ifs.”) Random assignment means nobody’s what-ifs get to decide which group they land in. Picture re-running the assignment over and over: on average, treatment and control would line up, because the assignment never peeked at anyone’s what-ifs. That is exchangeability: as far as the what-ifs go, the two groups are swappable.

(For the notation fans, that is the statement \(\bigl(Y^{(0)}, Y^{(1)}\bigr) \perp\!\!\!\perp T\): the what-ifs are independent of who got the treatment.)

Notice the catch. “Swappable on average across many possible assignments” is not the same as “identical in the one assignment you actually got.” Any single draw can put more eco-minded, price-sensitive, or high-income people into one group. And this is true whether you have 20 participants or 2,000: the guarantee comes from how people were assigned, not from how many there are.

So when two groups look different, there are two very different explanations:

  1. The assignment was flawed. Something let participants’ what-ifs influence who got which condition (self-selection, a predictable allocation sequence, a bug). That can undermine causal identification.
  2. The assignment was fine, and the groups differ by luck. That adds random variation to a valid estimate. It does not invalidate randomization.

So what does a valid randomization promise, and what does it leave open?

  • It does promise that assignment doesn’t depend on participants’ what-ifs, so across possible assignments the estimate is centered on the effect in your sample.
  • It doesn’t promise that the groups in your experiment are matched on everything, that your estimate is close to the true effect in your sample, that your DV measures only the construct you meant, that your sample represents the population you care about, or that a balance table can detect the differences that matter.

The rest of this page works through each of those in turn.

Question What answers it?
Was assignment genuinely random? How the assignment was actually done, which you document and check
Do the groups we got look similar? A plain description of their baseline characteristics
How much does a difference matter? How strongly those characteristics are tied to the outcome

This echoes Module 1: something you can see (a balance table) is not the whole story about the process behind it. But the assignment procedure isn’t a hidden construct you measure with error. It’s something you can and should verify directly.

Shuffling Cards: The Mechanism Versus the Hand You Receive

Consider how random assignment works when shuffling a deck of cards.

Was the deck shuffled properly? Start with a deck sorted by suit and shuffle it once, badly. A lot of the original order survives, so the shuffle didn’t get you anywhere near a random deck. A sloppy shuffle is the analogue of a flawed assignment. The question is how the order was produced, not whether one particular arrangement looks random.

What hands did we happen to get? Now deal a properly shuffled deck into two hands of 26. On average each hand gets two aces and 13 red cards. But with a perfectly valid shuffle, one hand can easily get three aces while the other gets one, or even all four.

Both hands have 26 cards and 13 red cards, so they’re perfectly balanced on those two things. But hand 1 holds 3 aces and hand 2 holds 1. With a valid shuffle, the aces split evenly 2–2 only 39% of the time, and all four end up in one hand 11% of the time. Lopsided hands don’t mean the shuffle was broken.

  • Good shuffle, uneven hands: valid mechanism, imbalance by luck.
  • Bad shuffle, even-looking hands: shaky mechanism, balance that looks reassuring.

Notice, too, that being balanced on one thing (red cards) tells you nothing about another (aces). If we’re playing a game where aces matter, the imbalance in aces matters. If we’re playing a game where only red cards matter, it may not. The same is true of characteristics that predict our experimental outcome.

Optional technical extension: how many riffle shuffles are enough?

For a real shuffle, the question “is it random yet?” has an exact answer (Bayer & Diaconis, 1992). The figure shows how far the order of a deck is from perfectly random after k riffle shuffles. A score of 0 means every ordering is equally likely; a score near 1 means the shuffle leaves the deck stuck in a small corner of all possible orders.

▶ Exact total variation distance for GSR riffle shuffles (code) 
# Eulerian counts E(n, r) for r = 1..n: the number of permutations of n cards with exactly
# r rising sequences. Recurrence: E(1,1) = 1; E(n,r) = r * E(n-1,r) + (n-r+1) * E(n-1,r-1),
# with values outside 1..n-1 equal to zero. The counts for each n sum to n!.
eulerian_counts <- function(n) {
  E <- 1
  if (n > 1) for (m in 2:n) {
    r     <- seq_len(m)
    prev  <- c(E, rep(0, m - length(E)))                    # E(m-1, r)
    prev1 <- c(0, E, rep(0, m - 1 - length(E)))             # E(m-1, r-1)
    E <- r * prev + (m - r + 1) * prev1
  }
  E
}

# Exact total variation distance from the uniform distribution on the n! orderings after k GSR
# riffle shuffles (Bayer & Diaconis, 1992). Probabilities are computed on the log scale.
riffle_tv <- function(n, k) {
  a    <- 2^k
  r    <- seq_len(n)
  P_r  <- exp(lchoose(a + n - r, n) - n * log(a))           # probability of ONE permutation with r rising sequences
  0.5 * sum(eulerian_counts(n) * abs(P_r - exp(-lgamma(n + 1))))
}

One riffle shuffle leaves even a 5-card deck far from random (distance 0.775). For 52 cards the distance is about 0.334 after seven shuffles, 0.167 after eight, and 0.085 after nine. The familiar “seven shuffles” rule marks the point where things start to settle down fast, not the point of perfect randomness, and the dashed 0.10 line is just an illustrative benchmark.

For the mathematically inclined: with \(a = 2^k\), a permutation of n cards with r rising sequences has probability \(\binom{a+n-r}{n}/a^{n}\) after k riffle shuffles, and the distance is \(\tfrac12\sum_{r=1}^{n} E(n,r)\,\bigl|P(n,k,r) - 1/n!\bigr|\), where \(E(n,r)\) counts permutations with r rising sequences. This is a property of the whole distribution of decks a shuffle can produce. You can’t read it off one deck, and a quick check like “is the deck still in its original order?” can look fine while the distribution is still far from random.

A correctly implemented randomization algorithm does not need additional attempts as the number of participant characteristics grows. What becomes harder is obtaining one realized assignment in which the groups are similar on every characteristic we care about. (A software sample() call draws the assignment directly from the intended distribution. Pressing “Randomize” again and again until the groups look good is not the same thing, and it isn’t automatically legitimate; pre-specified rerandomization is a distinct design, covered under “What Should Researchers Actually Do?” below.)

From Playing Cards to Psychological Constructs

Now let’s swap cards for people. Each participant has background characteristics that affect the outcome, the way each card has a colour and a rank. Below are two made-up scenarios. They aren’t claims that narrow outcomes always have fewer causes than broad psychological ones. They’re there to show what changes when a few characteristics matter versus many. And importantly, not all characteristics matter equally: some move the outcome a lot, some barely at all. Does that mean every experiment with 80 participants breaks the rules of an experiment, just because it can’t represent every profile? No. What matters is how those 80 participants end up distributed across the profiles, that is, the composition of the groups. This is worth taking seriously, because we rarely care only about the people in front of us. We study average treatment effects because we want to say something that generalizes. If chance imbalance on characteristics that matter is producing part of the difference we observe, that gets in the way of the effect we are actually after. Whether it does depends on how the groups are composed, and that is what the next sections are about.

Three different things people call “complexity”

It helps to separate three counts that tend to get blurred together:

  • the number of latent constructs the DV reflects,
  • the number of pre-treatment characteristics that predict the outcome, and
  • the number of combinations (profiles, interactions) of those characteristics.

More latent constructs doesn’t, by itself, make randomization invalid or make the estimate noisier. Each count matters in a different way, which the next sections take one at a time.

A DV can capture more than one construct

“Sustainable purchase intention” sounds like one thing. But a typical item (How likely are you to buy this eco-friendly product?) can blend at least two components: motivation to choose eco-friendly products, and practical willingness to do so given price, convenience, and quality. Different characteristics drive each: environmental values and social norms mostly feed motivation, while price sensitivity and household income mostly feed practical willingness.

The composite shows an effect of 0.3 SD. That same 0.3 would also appear if the treatment raised both components by 0.3. This brings two separate problems:

  1. Construct interpretation. A composite DV is not inherently invalid, but the effect on it doesn’t identify the effect on any one latent construct. This problem can exist even with perfectly balanced groups.
  2. Consequential imbalance. With more outcome-relevant characteristics, there are more ways for chance imbalance to matter. But more latent constructs does not mechanically mean more uncertainty. What matters is how much of the outcome’s variability the characteristics explain (see the simulation below).

From Exact Coverage to Approximate Orthogonality

We can’t cover every profile, so the goal becomes more modest: we want who got treated to be roughly unrelated to the characteristics that drive the outcome. (The technical name for “roughly unrelated” is approximately orthogonal.) Let’s see what that looks like.

Step A: make the imbalance concrete

▶ Correlation between treatment and a binary characteristic, from group counts 
# Correlation between treatment (exactly m treated, m control) and a binary characteristic, given
# that `a` treated and `b` control participants have X = 1. Constant characteristics (p = 0 or 1)
# have no group difference, so r = 0 rather than NaN.
treat_cov_cor <- function(a, b, m) {
  p1 <- a / m; p0 <- b / m; p <- (a + b) / (2 * m)
  ifelse(p == 0 | p == 1, 0, (p1 - p0) / (2 * sqrt(p * (1 - p))))
}
treat_cov_cor_ex <- treat_cov_cor(22, 18, 40)      # 55% vs. 45% high among 40 + 40 participants

Take 80 participants, 40 in each condition, and say exactly 40 of them have high environmental concern. Perfect balance would put 20 high-concern people in each group (50% vs. 50%). One perfectly valid assignment might put 22 in treatment and 18 in control: 55% vs. 45%. That’s a ten-percentage-point gap from moving two people. For a characteristic that half the sample has, with equal-sized groups, the correlation between treatment and the characteristic equals the gap in proportions, so here the correlation is r = 0.10.

We’ll call \(r_{\max}\) the biggest treatment-characteristic correlation we’re willing to put up with across the characteristics we check. Step B: more characteristics make strict balance less likely

Let’s make that exact under a deliberately simple setup: equal-sized groups, D characteristics that are independent of one another, each “high” for half the sample, a fixed tolerance of \(|r| < 0.10\), and valid randomization in every experiment.

▶ Exact probability of meeting the marginal-balance criterion (code) 
# q(N): probability that ONE independent Bernoulli(0.5) characteristic has |cor(T, X)| < thresh
# under complete randomization with N/2 per arm. The inequality is strict; exact ties
# (|r| = thresh) count as failing, with a tiny tolerance to guard against floating-point error.
q_balance <- function(N, thresh = 0.10) {
  stopifnot(N %% 2 == 0)
  m  <- N / 2
  pm <- dbinom(0:m, m, 0.5)                          # P(a) and P(b): Binomial(N/2, 0.5)
  g  <- expand.grid(a = 0:m, b = 0:m)
  r  <- treat_cov_cor(g$a, g$b, m)
  sum(pm[g$a + 1] * pm[g$b + 1] * (abs(r) < thresh - 1e-9))
}

# P(pass on all D independent characteristics) = q(N)^D
p_balance_all <- function(N, D, thresh = 0.10) q_balance(N, thresh)^D

At 80 participants the chance of meeting the criterion on every characteristic is 32.0% for two characteristics and 0.3% for ten. More participants make strict balance easier; more characteristics make it harder. These aren’t the probabilities that randomization “worked” or that exchangeability holds: the assignment is valid throughout. They also rest on the simple setup above, so they aren’t universal sample-size rules. (The math behind them: for each possible pair of counts of “high” people in the two groups we work out the correlation and its probability, add up the pairs with \(|r| < 0.10\), and raise the one-characteristic answer to the power D.)

What these probabilities are not: they aren’t the probability that randomization “worked,” that the estimate is unbiased, that the sample-level effect is identified, or that your estimate is close to the truth. And \(2^D\) counts the possible joint profiles (1,024 for ten characteristics); it isn’t a sample size you would need.

When Does Imbalance Actually Matter?

A gap between the groups matters when it is a gap on something that moves the outcome. Take environmental values (a strong predictor, 0.80 SD) and age (a weak one, 0.05 SD). If the groups differ on either by 10 percentage points, chance imbalance on environmental values adds about 0.08 SD to the difference we observe, while the same gap on age adds only 0.005 SD. So imbalance on an irrelevant characteristic is nearly harmless, and imbalance on a strong predictor can move the estimate a lot.

Let’s watch that happen. We take the same 80 participants, with the ten characteristics from the table above, and give the treatment a known effect of 0.40 SD for everyone. Then we randomize those 80 people over and over, 4,000 times. Every one of those assignments is valid.

  • What is randomized: which 40 of the 80 participants get treatment.
  • What is held fixed: the 80 participants, their characteristics, and both of their potential outcomes, so the true effect in this sample (the sample average treatment effect) is exactly 0.40 every time.
  • What we measure: the difference in mean outcomes between groups (“unadjusted”), and the same estimate after adjusting for the ten pre-treatment characteristics, which is a pre-planned analysis.

NoteTechnical note: why the error is the imbalance, and does having more characteristics widen the spread?

With a constant treatment effect \(\tau\) and outcome \(Y = \tau T + \sum_j \beta_j C_j + \varepsilon\), the unadjusted estimate equals \(\tau + \sum_j \beta_j(\bar C_{j,T} - \bar C_{j,C}) + (\bar\varepsilon_T - \bar\varepsilon_C)\): the treatment effect, plus imbalance weighted by each characteristic’s effect, plus leftover noise.

Does adding characteristics make estimates less precise? Not by itself. In a completely randomized experiment with equal groups the spread of the unadjusted estimate is proportional to the SD of the untreated outcome, whatever produced that variability. The table checks this with the same 80 participants and the same 4,000 assignments throughout.

What are we actually estimating?

There are two different gaps between “the number we got” and “the thing we care about,” and they have different causes.

  • Assignment uncertainty. The sample average treatment effect (SATE) is fixed by who is in the study and what their outcomes would be under each condition. Random assignment doesn’t change it. It decides which participants end up treated, so it determines how far the observed estimate lands from the SATE. That is the spread you just saw.
  • Sampling and generalization uncertainty. The SATE can differ from the population average effect because the participants may not represent the people we care about. Suppose a sustainability intervention works strongly for environmentally concerned consumers (effect 0.8) and weakly for everyone else (0.2). If 60% of our sample is environmentally concerned but only 30% of the target population is, the sample effect is 0.56 while the population effect is 0.38, even with perfectly balanced treatment and control groups. Measurement can differ across populations too, so the same observed DV may not mean the same thing everywhere.

Keep these apart from confounding. Under a valid randomization, chance imbalance is a mean-zero wobble that adds uncertainty; the algebra above is the same algebra as omitted-variable bias in Module 1, Part 2, but it’s not the systematic bias you get when assignment itself depends on a characteristic (say, the eco-label version advertised on an environmental forum and the control version not).

Randomization buys us a big shortcut: we can compare the groups without measuring everything else in the data-generating process. That shortcut rests on the characteristics that matter being reasonably balanced in our sample. When they aren’t, or when we can’t tell, measuring the important ones lets us check balance and adjust. That is why complex outcomes, with many predictors and many possible combinations, ask more of our sample size and our measurement.

When the characteristics are correlated

So far the characteristics have been independent of each other. In real data they aren’t: people who care about the environment also tend to care about health, social norms, and so on. Correlation doesn’t automatically help or hurt, and correlated predictors aren’t each an independent source of confounding. The simulation asks two questions: how often is a strict balance rule met, and how big is the swing that chance imbalance adds to the estimate? Why Detecting an Effect Doesn’t Establish Comparability

A study can produce statistically detectable evidence of an outcome difference while offering limited evidence about whether its realized groups are balanced on important characteristics, and conventional balance diagnostics can have little ability to spot modest problems in how assignment was implemented. We’ll look at two scenarios, being careful about what each one shows.

Scenario A: valid randomization

Here assignment is a correct complete randomization, 80 participants, the ten characteristics from the table, and a real treatment effect of 0.6 SD.

Scenario B: a defective assignment

Now suppose assignment is slightly defective. Each participant’s chance of treatment rises modestly with a pre-treatment characteristic \(U\) (think environmental values) that also drives the outcome. We have a noisy measure \(W\) of \(U\), as researchers usually do, and we run the usual balance diagnostic (does \(W\) predict treatment?). The true treatment effect is zero, so any significant treatment test is a false positive.

  • Assignment: each person is treated with probability that increases by 0.3 log-odds per SD of \(U\) (a modest defect).
  • Outcome: \(Y = 0.8\,U + \text{noise}\) (SD 1), with no treatment effect.
  • Measured: a proxy \(W = U + \text{noise}\), in two qualities: reliability 0.6 (decent) and 0.3 (poor).
  • Statistics: the ordinary unadjusted treatment test (false-positive rate), and a logistic-regression diagnostic of treatment on \(W\) (detection rate).

With this modest defect, the unadjusted estimate is off by about 0.23 SD at every sample size, so the false-positive rate climbs steadily with N: 21% at N = 160 and 64% at N = 640, against a nominal 5%. How well the diagnostic can notice the problem depends on N, on the size of the defect, and on how well the characteristic was measured. With a decent proxy it flags the defect somewhat more often than the treatment test falsely rejects; with a poor proxy it lags behind (17% versus 21% at N = 160, 52% versus 64% at N = 640). At small samples neither does much: at N = 80 even the decent proxy flags the defect in only 14% of experiments. And for a characteristic nobody measured, there is nothing to run the diagnostic on.

What this does and doesn’t show:

  • Detecting a treatment-outcome association doesn’t prove the assignment was valid.
  • A non-significant balance diagnostic doesn’t prove equivalence, and it can’t evaluate an unmeasured characteristic.
  • The power of a diagnostic depends on N, the size of the defect, and measurement quality.
  • None of this is a universal rule that effects are easier to detect than assignment problems. It shows conditions under which a convincing-looking effect can coexist with weak evidence about assignment quality.
  • And a valid randomization doesn’t become invalid because a balance test happens to reject.

What Should Researchers Actually Do?

The checks you need depend on the claim you want to make. Randomization gives you a valid basis for causal inference; the work is in making sure the rest of the chain holds.

Before collecting data

  • Pin down the construct and the DV. What theoretical construct do you want to study, and does the planned DV measure it, or could it mix several?
  • Identify the major pre-treatment predictors of the outcome and plausible moderators. Use theory, past research, and pilot data. You don’t need to measure everything that might matter, and you don’t need to split participants into every possible profile; you need the characteristics that matter most.
  • Ask whether your sample is relevant to the population you care about.
  • Run a power analysis for the effect and estimand you actually want.
  • Consider blocking or stratifying on important predictors (Module 2, Part 5), and pre-specify a covariate-adjustment strategy when it helps.
  • Document the assignment mechanism, and plan for dropout, non-compliance, and other post-assignment selection.
  • If you intend to rerandomize, pre-specify the balance criterion and analyze accordingly (Morgan & Rubin, 2012). Pressing “Randomize” until the groups look good is not automatically legitimate.

After collecting data

  • Audit that assignment was implemented as intended (logs, deviations, group sizes).
  • Describe observed baseline differences for characteristics measured before treatment, without treating p-values as a certification of randomization. Judge a difference by how strongly that characteristic relates to the outcome.
  • Follow the planned covariate-adjusted analysis, where appropriate. Adjusting for measured variables doesn’t repair a flawed assignment, doesn’t remove unmeasured confounding, and doesn’t make an effect transport to a different population.
  • Look for differential attrition or exclusions.
  • Separate the effect on the observed DV from the effect on the theoretical construct.
  • Separate conclusions about the sample from conclusions about the target population, and be appropriately cautious where construct interpretation, sampling, or implementation can’t be supported.

When you can’t check what you can’t see

A balance table only covers what you measured. Pilot data and open-ended responses (see Part 4) can help you list candidate characteristics measured before treatment that you could block on or adjust for. Keep those separate from mediators, which treatment can change: don’t balance-check or adjust for mediators when you want the total effect. A mediator list becomes relevant for studying pathways, and even then measuring mediators doesn’t establish their causal effects (Module 3, Part 4).

Researcher Checklist: Exchangeability

NoteKey questions about the strength of your causal claim

Randomization is powerful because, when implemented correctly, it breaks the systematic relationship between treatment assignment and participants’ potential outcomes. But that guarantee doesn’t mean the groups in our particular experiment are identical, or that the result automatically answers our broader research question.

This matters especially when our DV reflects several underlying constructs. Different characteristics may determine different components of the outcome, and some of those characteristics may be unevenly distributed across the groups we happened to draw. Those differences can affect the precision of our estimate, while the mixture of constructs can complicate what that estimate actually means.

Nor does a balance table solve the problem. It only examines characteristics we measured, and finding no statistically significant differences doesn’t establish equivalence. Conversely, observing imbalance doesn’t invalidate a correctly randomized experiment.

The goal, then, isn’t to prove that every possible characteristic is perfectly balanced. It’s to understand what randomization guarantees, what remains uncertain in the realized experiment, whether the DV represents the construct of interest, and how far the evidence supports the conclusion we want to draw.
