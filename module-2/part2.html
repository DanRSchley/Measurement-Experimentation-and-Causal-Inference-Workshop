Part 2: What Does “Random Assignment” Actually Require?

WarningThe central message of Part 2

A valid random-assignment mechanism establishes independence by design. It does not guarantee that the groups in any one experiment are balanced. Random assignment is a procedure — clicking “Randomize” in Qualtrics, running sample() in R. If the procedure is implemented correctly, treatment is independent of every pre-treatment characteristic and of every potential outcome. That independence is what makes the groups exchangeable in the sense that licenses causal inference, and it holds no matter how many characteristics affect Y. But exchangeability is a property of the assignment mechanism, defined over hypothetical repetitions of the assignment. In one realized experiment the two groups will almost always differ somewhat, on measured and unmeasured characteristics alike, and the more characteristics matter, the less likely it is that all of them are well balanced at once.

This part separates four ideas: (1) the randomness of the assignment mechanism, (2) balance in expectation, (3) realized balance in one finite sample, and (4) balance on single characteristics versus balance on their joint distribution.

NoteThree reasons treatment groups can differ

Not all differences between groups have the same cause or the same remedy:

  1. A flawed assignment mechanism — the procedure itself was not truly random (e.g., participants self-selected into conditions, blocks were allocated predictably, or the algorithm had a bug). The difference is structural and systematic; better analysis cannot fix a broken procedure.
  2. A valid randomization with realized imbalance on measured characteristics — the procedure was correct, but any finite-sample draw will produce some imbalance by chance. A balance table describes the realized sample; it does not grade the procedure.
  3. Realized imbalance on unmeasured characteristics — a special case of the second reason that no balance table can reveal, because the relevant characteristics were never measured.

The right language for cases 2 and 3 is “realized imbalance,” not “randomization failed.” Chance imbalance does not make a valid procedure invalid, does not by itself bias the estimator (it averages to zero over repetitions of the assignment), and is already reflected in the estimator’s sampling variability. What it can do is move this particular estimate away from the effect you care about, and it matters more when the imbalanced characteristic strongly predicts Y. Whether a given imbalance matters depends on how the characteristic relates to the outcome.

The Measurement Analogy: What You Observe vs. What You Want to Know

In Module 1, we introduced a distinction that runs through all of empirical research:

Example from Module 1
Observable (indicator) A participant’s response on a 7-point Likert scale
Latent construct Their true level of Green Purchase Intention

The observable is what you can see. The latent construct is what you actually want to know. The entire Module 1 was about the gap between these two things: your observed scale picks up variance from the wrong latent constructs (discriminant validity), from unequal measurement properties across groups (non-invariance), and from subpopulations that behave differently (latent classes).

The same distinction helps with randomization, with one important difference. There are two different things to look at, and they are checked in different ways:

What you can verify or observe What you want to be able to say
The assignment mechanism How assignment was actually implemented: a documented random number generator, concealed allocation, no deviations from the allocation sequence Assignment is independent of potential outcomes — exchangeability by design
The realized sample Balance tables, standardized mean differences, and other descriptions of how the two groups look on the characteristics you measured How similar the groups are on the characteristics that drive Y — including the ones you did not measure

Unlike a Likert item, which is an imperfect indicator of an attitude you can never see directly, a correctly implemented random mechanism is a design guarantee, not a noisy measurement of exchangeability. The measurement problem arises one step later: your sample is a finite draw from that mechanism, and what you can inspect about it (the measured covariates) is only part of what matters for Y.

ImportantTwo questions to keep separate
  1. Was the assignment mechanism a valid random procedure? Answer this from how the experiment was run — software, allocation concealment, protocol deviations — not from a balance table.
  2. How balanced did this particular draw turn out to be? Answer this descriptively, for the characteristics you measured, and ask whether any imbalance is large relative to how strongly those characteristics predict Y.

A balanced table does not certify the first, and an imbalanced table does not refute it.

A Concrete Anchor: The Playing Cards Analogy

Imagine a deck of cards in a known order. Before a game, you shuffle it. Did the shuffle make the deck random?

“Random” has a precise meaning here: each of the n! possible orderings is equally likely. The usual way to shuffle by hand is a riffle shuffle — cut the deck in two and interleave the halves — and its standard mathematical model is the Gilbert–Shannon–Reeds (GSR) model, whose behaviour is known exactly (Bayer & Diaconis, 1992). We can ask how far the distribution of deck orders produced by k riffle shuffles is from the uniform distribution over all n! orderings.

The natural measure of that distance is the total variation (TV) distance: the largest difference, over any collection of orderings, between the probability the shuffling procedure assigns to it and the probability a perfectly random deck would. TV = 0 means the procedure produces every ordering with equal probability. TV near 1 means the procedure concentrates its probability on a small fraction of the orderings.

NoteWhy not just check how correlated the shuffled deck is with the original order?

A natural check is whether a shuffled deck still resembles its original order (for example, Kendall’s τ). That is a statistic of one deck. A shuffling procedure can produce decks with almost no correlation with the original order and still be far from uniform, because the distribution can retain a great deal of structure that one correlation does not reveal. TV distance is a property of the whole distribution the procedure generates, which is what “random” refers to.

For n cards and k riffle shuffles, let \(a = 2^k\). Under the GSR model, any particular permutation with r rising sequences has probability

\[P(n,k,r) = \frac{\binom{a+n-r}{n}}{a^{n}}.\]

If \(E(n,r)\) is the number of permutations of n cards with exactly r rising sequences (an Eulerian number), the exact total variation distance from uniform is

\[\mathrm{TV}(n,k) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{r=1}^{n} E(n,r)\,\left|P(n,k,r) - \frac{1}{n!}\right|.\]

This is a deterministic calculation — no simulation is needed.

▶ Exact total variation distance for GSR riffle shuffles 
# Eulerian counts E(n, r) for r = 1..n: the number of permutations of n cards with exactly
# r rising sequences. Recurrence: E(1,1) = 1; E(n,r) = r * E(n-1,r) + (n-r+1) * E(n-1,r-1),
# with values outside 1..n-1 equal to zero. The counts for each n sum to n!.
eulerian_counts <- function(n) {
  E <- 1
  if (n > 1) for (m in 2:n) {
    r     <- seq_len(m)
    prev  <- c(E, rep(0, m - length(E)))                    # E(m-1, r)
    prev1 <- c(0, E, rep(0, m - 1 - length(E)))             # E(m-1, r-1)
    E <- r * prev + (m - r + 1) * prev1
  }
  E
}

# Exact total variation distance from the uniform distribution on the n! orderings after k GSR
# riffle shuffles (Bayer & Diaconis, 1992). Probabilities are computed on the log scale.
riffle_tv <- function(n, k) {
  a    <- 2^k
  r    <- seq_len(n)
  P_r  <- exp(lchoose(a + n - r, n) - n * log(a))           # probability of ONE permutation with r rising sequences
  0.5 * sum(eulerian_counts(n) * abs(P_r - exp(-lgamma(n + 1))))
}

One riffle shuffle does not make even a small deck random: after a single shuffle a 5-card deck is still at TV = 0.775 from uniform. The familiar seven-shuffle result for a 52-card deck is not a claim of perfect uniformity either. The distance after seven shuffles is about 0.334, after eight about 0.167, and after nine about 0.085. Seven shuffles marks the rapid-mixing transition — the distance collapses from near 1 to a few tenths and then falls geometrically — not a threshold at which randomness suddenly becomes complete.

What the card example does — and does not — show

The deck has n! possible orderings, and a limited randomizing process (a few riffle shuffles) can leave important structure in the distribution of orderings even when a simple summary of one deck makes it look random. Larger decks need more shuffles under this particular mixing model. That is the intuition worth keeping: checking one limited feature of randomness is not the same as examining the whole distribution.

But the card problem is not the same as random treatment assignment, and the analogy should not be pushed literally. Deck size is not the dimensionality of an outcome, and the number of shuffles is not the number of times Qualtrics must randomize. A correct assignment algorithm draws the assignment from its intended distribution in one step (sample() draws a uniformly random subset directly); it does not need more applications as the number of outcome-relevant characteristics grows. The assignment mechanism is valid however many characteristics there are.

What does grow with the number of characteristics is a different quantity: how hard it is for one valid draw to look balanced on all of them at once. That is the question that matters for the experiment:

As the number of outcome-relevant characteristics increases, how difficult is it for one valid random assignment to produce treatment and control groups that satisfy a demanding balance criterion across all of them?

From Shuffling Cards to Balancing Experimental Groups

Here is a deliberately simple experiment in which the assignment mechanism is valid in every scenario:

  • There are N participants (N even). Each has D binary pre-treatment characteristics. Think of them as hypothetical baseline characteristics that might predict the outcome (for example, high vs. low environmental values), not as dimensions of the outcome measure itself.
  • Each characteristic is generated independently with P(X = 1) = 0.5.
  • Treatment is assigned by complete randomization: exactly N/2 participants are treated and N/2 are controls, independently of the characteristics.

Because assignment is valid, the groups are exchangeable by design. The question is what one realized draw looks like. For each characteristic, compute the correlation between treatment assignment and that characteristic. Call a draw marginally balanced at the illustrative tolerance of 0.10 if

\[\max_{j=1,\dots,D}\,\bigl|\mathrm{cor}(T, X_j)\bigr| < 0.10 .\]

This is a descriptive criterion for this exercise. It is not a test of whether random assignment was valid, and 0.10 is not a universal scientific cutoff.

An exact calculation. With N/2 participants per arm and independent Bernoulli(0.5) characteristics, the number of participants with X = 1 in each arm follows a Binomial(N/2, 0.5) distribution. For every pair of counts we can compute the treatment–characteristic correlation and its probability. With group proportions \(p_1\) and \(p_0\) and pooled proportion \(p\), the correlation for equal-sized arms is

\[r = \frac{p_1 - p_0}{2\sqrt{p\,(1-p)}} .\]

If \(p = 0\) or \(p = 1\) the characteristic is constant, there is no observed group difference, and it is not treated as a failed balance check. Summing the probabilities of all count pairs with |r| < 0.10 gives q(N), the probability of passing the criterion on one characteristic. Because characteristics are independent, the probability of passing on all D is q(N)D.

▶ Exact probability of meeting the marginal-balance criterion 
# Correlation between treatment (exactly m treated, m control) and a binary characteristic, given
# that `a` treated and `b` control participants have X = 1. Constant characteristics (p = 0 or 1)
# have no group difference, so r = 0 rather than NaN.
treat_cov_cor <- function(a, b, m) {
  p1 <- a / m; p0 <- b / m; p <- (a + b) / (2 * m)
  ifelse(p == 0 | p == 1, 0, (p1 - p0) / (2 * sqrt(p * (1 - p))))
}

# q(N): probability that ONE independent Bernoulli(0.5) characteristic has |cor(T, X)| < thresh
# under complete randomization with N/2 per arm. The inequality is strict; exact ties
# (|r| = thresh) count as failing, with a tiny tolerance to guard against floating-point error.
q_balance <- function(N, thresh = 0.10) {
  stopifnot(N %% 2 == 0)
  m  <- N / 2
  pm <- dbinom(0:m, m, 0.5)                          # P(a) and P(b): Binomial(N/2, 0.5)
  g  <- expand.grid(a = 0:m, b = 0:m)
  r  <- treat_cov_cor(g$a, g$b, m)
  sum(pm[g$a + 1] * pm[g$b + 1] * (abs(r) < thresh - 1e-9))
}

# P(pass on all D independent characteristics) = q(N)^D
p_balance_all <- function(N, D, thresh = 0.10) q_balance(N, thresh)^D

What the table and graph show:

  • Assignment is genuinely randomized in every scenario. Nothing here is a defective procedure.
  • At a fixed sample size, meeting the same simultaneous criterion is much less likely when more independent characteristics are considered. With 80 participants the probability of meeting the criterion on two characteristics is about 32%; on ten characteristics it is about 0.3%.
  • Increasing N makes a demanding realized-balance criterion easier to meet.
  • Not meeting the criterion does not mean the randomization was invalid. It is what a valid mechanism will do, with the stated probability.

These are results from a stylized model — independent, binary, equally prevalent characteristics and one particular tolerance — not universal sample-size requirements. And a draw that misses the criterion is not necessarily a problem: whether realized imbalance matters depends on how strongly the characteristics predict the outcome.

Marginal Balance Is Not Joint Balance

Both groups have the same mean on A (0.5) and the same mean on B (0.5), so a balance table that looks at one characteristic at a time would show perfect balance. Yet their joint distributions are completely different. If untreated outcomes depend on the interaction of A and B (here, Y(0) = 1 when A and B match and 0 otherwise), the treatment group has a mean untreated potential outcome of 1 and the control group has 0.

This is a deliberately extreme illustration, not a claim that such a configuration is typical. Its purpose is to show that balancing individual characteristics does not guarantee balancing their joint distribution, or the potential outcomes. It also connects to the 2D participant profiles discussed below: the number of joint profiles grows quickly with D. That count describes how many ways a sample can differ; it does not mean a valid randomized experiment must observe or balance every possible profile.

Exchangeability: What Random Assignment Establishes

The permutation logic in Part 1 rested on exchangeability: if treatment had no effect, we could have assigned any participant to either condition without changing what we would observe. In formal terms, exchangeability of the potential outcomes with respect to treatment means:

\[Y^{(0)}, Y^{(1)} \perp\!\!\!\perp T\]

That is, potential outcomes (what Y would be under control, what Y would be under treatment) are independent of which condition a participant actually received. This is the statistical expression of “the groups have no pre-existing differences that matter, on average, across repetitions of the assignment.”

Three ideas need to be kept apart:

Design-based exchangeability. When treatment is assigned independently of potential outcomes through a valid randomization mechanism, the independence condition is supplied by the research design. It does not depend on how many characteristics affect Y, and it is what justifies randomization-based inference.

Realized balance. A particular randomized allocation can produce treatment and control groups that differ on observed or unobserved characteristics. These realized differences affect the observed treatment contrast, its precision, and — conditional on the draw — how far the estimate sits from the effect of interest. They do not mean the mechanism was invalid.

Dimensionality. As the number of characteristics or relevant interactions increases, it becomes harder for any single draw to satisfy a demanding simultaneous balance requirement. Whether the resulting imbalances meaningfully affect estimation depends on how those characteristics relate to potential outcomes.

Two things this does not say. It does not say that every nonzero sample correlation is a failure of randomization, identification, or validity. And it does not say that a randomized experiment can be declared valid or invalid by empirical balance testing alone.

In practice, what a researcher can do is separate two kinds of evidence:

  • About the mechanism: how assignment was implemented — documented random assignment, concealed allocation, no protocol deviations. This is verified by process.
  • About the realized sample: how the groups turned out on the characteristics you measured. This is described by balance diagnostics, and it says nothing directly about characteristics you did not measure.

How much realized imbalance you should expect, and whether it matters, depends on two things: 1. How many characteristics affect Y, and how strongly (the complexity of the construct) 2. How many participants you have (the sample size)

From Exact Coverage to Approximate Orthogonality

Here is the key insight: a valid randomized experiment does not need to fill every cell. What matters for any given outcome is how far the realized treatment groups differ on the characteristics that affect it.

With random assignment and N participants, the expected correlation between treatment T and any single characteristic C is zero. The realized correlation in any given sample deviates from zero by chance, with standard error ≈ 1 / √N. If we want the realized correlations on ALL D characteristics to stay below a tolerance \(|r_{\max}|\) with probability at least \(1-\alpha\), a Bonferroni argument over the D simultaneous comparisons gives the approximate sample size

\[N \gtrsim \left(\frac{z_{1-\alpha/(2D)}}{r_{\max}}\right)^2 .\]

This is a conservative planning approximation under stated assumptions (independent characteristics and a normal approximation for the correlation). It says how large N must be for a valid randomization to typically meet a chosen balance tolerance on every characteristic. It is not the number of participants needed to verify that randomization was valid. “From Shuffling Cards to Balancing Experimental Groups” computed the corresponding probabilities exactly for binary characteristics.

What does \(r_{\max}\) actually mean?

\(r_{\max}\) is a tolerance for realized imbalance, expressed as a correlation between treatment and a characteristic. To see why the size of that imbalance matters, suppose outcomes follow an additive model with a constant treatment effect \(\tau\):

\[Y = \tau T + \beta_C\, C + \varepsilon .\]

The observed difference in group means is then

\[\bar Y_T - \bar Y_C \;=\; \tau \;+\; \underbrace{\beta_C\,(\bar C_T - \bar C_C)}_{\text{prognostic contribution of realized imbalance}} \;+\; (\bar\varepsilon_T - \bar\varepsilon_C).\]

The middle term is the effect of C on Y times the realized difference in C between the groups, in whatever units the model uses. Over repeated valid randomizations its expected value is zero, so it is not bias in the estimator; it is realized variability, and it is part of what the standard error describes. But in the draw you actually got, it moves your estimate away from \(\tau\).

For a binary characteristic with prevalence 0.5 and equal-sized arms, the realized correlation equals the difference in proportions, so the contribution is simply \(\beta_C \times r_{T,C}\), where \(\beta_C\) is the outcome difference between the high and low levels of C. (For other characteristics the scaling differs; the contribution is always \(\beta_C\) times the realized mean difference in C.) We express \(\beta_C\) in SD units of Y, which is identical to Cohen’s d when Y is standardized.

A quick reminder from the power table in Part 1: Cohen’s d expresses the treatment effect in the same SD-unit currency. An effect of d = 0.40 means the group means differ by 0.40 standard deviations of Y. The β values below use that same ruler — so a β of 0.50 means the characteristic shifts Y by half a standard deviation.

Realized Imbalance, Flawed Assignment, and Omitted Variable Bias

The additive algebra above is the same algebra as omitted variable bias in Module 1, Part 2. What differs is the status of the middle term, and that depends on how the groups came to differ:

  • When assignment depends on C — an observational comparison, self-selection, or a flawed assignment mechanism — the expected difference in C is not zero, so the term is a systematic bias: omitted variable bias in the usual sense.
  • When assignment is a valid randomization, the expected difference in C is zero. The term is then a mean-zero realized deviation — part of the estimator’s sampling variability, reduced by larger samples, by blocking, or by adjusting for C when it is measured — and not bias in the estimator.

A concrete example of the first case. Suppose the eco-label version of the study was advertised on an environmental forum, while the control version ran through a general panel, so that assignment is related to environmental values:

Group High env. values (%) Observed WTP
Control (N = 95) 38% $4.20
Eco-label (N = 105) 61% $5.50

The share of high-environmental-values participants differs by 0.23 between groups, and high environmental values raise WTP by about β ≈ 0.8 SD (≈ $1.20) even without any eco-label stimulus.

What you observe: Eco-label group mean WTP = $5.50, control mean = $4.20. Observed difference = $1.30.

The contribution: \(\beta_C \times (\bar C_T - \bar C_C) = 1.20 \times 0.23 \approx \$0.28\).

Eco-label effect net of that contribution: $1.30 − $0.28 = $1.02 — the raw estimate is inflated by roughly 27%.

Here the groups are not exchangeable, because they were not formed by a valid randomization: assignment depended on environmental values. You are comparing “eco-label + environmentally minded participants” against “no eco-label + less environmentally minded participants.” Had the same participants instead been randomized, the expected difference in environmental values would have been zero, and only a smaller, mean-zero chance contribution would remain.

TipReading the direction of the contribution from a balance table

Because the contribution is \(\beta_C \times\) (realized difference in C), you can reason about its direction for any characteristic you measured:

  • Treated group has more of a positively prognostic characteristic (difference > 0, β > 0): the contribution is positive — the observed effect looks larger than \(\tau\) in this draw
  • Treated group has less of a positively prognostic characteristic (difference < 0, β > 0): the contribution is negative — the effect looks smaller
  • The characteristic has a negative effect on Y (β < 0): these directions flip

For characteristics you did not measure, the direction is unknown. That is why preventing imbalance by design (blocked randomization, larger N) and adjusting for measured prognostic covariates are preferable to hoping the draw was lucky.

This is why \(r_{\max}\) is a useful tolerance: it limits how much a realized imbalance can contribute to the observed difference for a given \(\beta\). A looser tolerance allows larger deviations; a stricter one requires more observations before a valid randomization will typically meet it.

At first glance, a row like D = 10 looks paradoxical: the Exact cells (2^D) = 1,024 column is smaller than the r_max = 0.05 column (≈ 3,152). The two columns answer different questions:

  • Exact cells (2^D): the number of possible profiles of D binary characteristics. It is also the size of a fully structured full-factorial assignment in which every profile appears once. By construction every characteristic is perfectly balanced and the characteristics are uncorrelated with each other, so no statistical question arises. But one participant per cell cannot support estimating variance: a realistic full factorial at D = 10 needs at least 10–30 observations per cell, putting the total at 10,240–30,720.

  • r_max columns: the N at which a random, unstructured assignment will typically (about 95% of the time, under the approximation) meet the tolerance on all D characteristics. With random assignment there is no structural guarantee of balance in any one draw; the guarantee holds in expectation.

So the exact-cells column tells you how large the space of profiles is, and the r_max columns tell you how many participants random assignment typically needs to look balanced to a given tolerance. A structured design (blocking or stratifying on characteristics you know about) can guarantee balance on those characteristics, at the price of measuring them in advance.

WarningThe key tradeoff: stricter balance criteria need many more observations

Lower \(r_{\max}\) = a stricter standard for realized balance = a smaller possible contribution from chance imbalance.

But lower \(r_{\max}\) also requires far more observations before a valid randomization will typically meet it. N scales as \(1/r_{\max}^2\), so halving your tolerance quadruples the required sample.

ImportantThe core punchline
  • Set a strict \(r_{\max}\) (e.g., 0.05–0.10) across many characteristics: a valid randomization will typically meet it only at sample sizes few lab studies can afford.
  • Accept a lenient \(r_{\max}\) (e.g., 0.30–0.40): achievable sample sizes, but any single draw can carry a noticeable contribution from chance imbalance when individual characteristics have strong effects (β ≈ 0.5–1.0 SD) on Y.

A typical psychology or marketing experiment with N = 60–120 participants will often miss tight simultaneous balance criteria across many characteristics, even though its assignment is valid. That is the expected behaviour of a valid mechanism in a small sample, not a defect. The practical implication is that, with many strongly prognostic characteristics, small samples rely on the standard error to describe their variability, and design choices (blocking on the key characteristics) or analysis choices (adjusting for measured ones) can tighten the estimate.

With a narrow outcome like Visual Fixation Time (D = 2 hypothetical characteristics):

  • For a ≥95% probability that |r| < 0.10 on both: N ≈ 503 — feasible
  • For |r| < 0.20 on both: N ≈ 126

With a broad outcome like Sustainable Purchase Intention (D = 10 hypothetical characteristics):

  • For a ≥95% probability that |r| < 0.10 on all ten: N ≈ 788 — rarely achieved in practice
  • For |r| < 0.20: N ≈ 197 — still demanding for most lab studies
  • A typical lab study with N = 80 has an effective tolerance of about 0.31: with roughly 95% probability the largest realized correlation is below that. In an unlucky draw, a characteristic with β ≈ 0.8 SD can then shift the observed difference by about 0.25 SD.

This is the direct experimental parallel to Module 1’s core finding about discriminant validity. A scale with many correlated sub-dimensions is hard to measure cleanly; a construct with many latent antecedents is hard to balance on every antecedent in one finite draw. Both problems grow with construct breadth, and both call for mapping the latent space before claiming clean measurement or clean identification.

Simulation: Correlated Characteristics — Two Countervailing Forces

The analysis so far assumed that the 10 hypothetical characteristics are independent of one another. But is that realistic?

Think about the characteristics that might drive Sustainable Purchase Intention. Environmental values, health motivation, social norm salience, and peer influence tend to cluster together — the “conscious consumer” profile. Similarly, price sensitivity, income, and habitual spending patterns cluster together — the “pragmatic buyer” profile. The characteristics are correlated, not orthogonal.

Two forces pull in opposite directions

Before running any simulation, consider the logic:

Force 1 — fewer independent checks (sometimes helps). If two characteristics are highly correlated (say, environmental values and social norm salience move together), then for the purpose of counting how many independent things can differ between the groups, you effectively have fewer than 10. Fewer independent checks means a lower probability that any one of them exceeds the tolerance by chance, so the balance criterion is slightly easier to meet for a given N.

Force 2 — correlated imbalances accumulate. The flip side is that when realized imbalance does occur on one characteristic, it tends to occur on the correlated ones too. Imbalance on “environmental values” drags along imbalance on “social norms,” “health motivation,” and “peer influence” at the same time. Each has its own effect β on Y, so their contributions to the observed treatment contrast add up in the same direction instead of cancelling.

When does correlation help vs. hurt?

The key variable is why the characteristics are correlated, and how strongly each predicts Y:

Scenario Structure Effect on realized balance
Helpful: characteristics cluster around a few latent “super-factors” with small effects e.g., all “values-based” characteristics move together as one bloc, each with a modest effect on Y Fewer effective checks; the criterion is easier to meet, and any imbalance contributes little
Hurtful: characteristics are correlated and each carries independent variance in Y e.g., environmental values, health focus, and social norms each have separate β effects on WTP One draw’s imbalance shows up on several characteristics at once, with additive contributions
Worst case: high correlation and strong independent effects on Y The characteristics are correlated with each other AND each has a large β One draw’s imbalance can contribute a large amount to the observed contrast

The concrete intuition: suppose you check environmental values and find it balanced. If it is highly correlated with health focus and social norms, those are probably balanced too — the helpful version. But if each carries an independent effect on WTP that environmental values do not capture, then an imbalance that does occur can show up on all three at once — the hurtful version.

This maps onto Module 1’s concept of correlated subscales. When subscales are highly correlated, you gain efficiency (fewer items capture the same information). But if they are correlated and carry separate predictive variance, then a problem in one cascades to all.

We run three scenarios, always with valid complete randomization (exactly N/2 treated) and a true treatment effect of zero:

  • Independent (ρ = 0, large varied β): the benchmark from the previous section.
  • Scenario A — tight clusters (ρ = 0.75, uniform small β = 0.15): characteristics are very strongly correlated — effectively 10 measured characteristics but only a few independent blocs — each with a small effect on Y.
  • Scenario B — moderate correlation (ρ = 0.35, heterogeneous large β from 0.05 to 1.50): characteristics are moderately correlated and each carries a different, large effect on Y.

For each experiment we record (i) whether it meets the marginal-balance criterion (max |cor| < 0.10), (ii) the realized prognostic contribution — how much chance imbalance moves the observed treatment contrast, \(\sum_j \beta_j(\bar X_{j,\text{treated}} - \bar X_{j,\text{control}})\) — and (iii) whether the ordinary t-test (and the same test after adjusting for the measured characteristics) falsely rejects.

# ── Helper: correlated binary covariates via Cholesky ────────────────────────
corr_binary <- function(N, D, rho) {
  Sigma <- matrix(rho, D, D); diag(Sigma) <- 1
  z     <- matrix(rnorm(N * D), nrow = N) %*% chol(Sigma)
  (z > 0L) * 1L
}
draw_X <- function(N, D, rho) {
  if (rho == 0) matrix(rbinom(N * D, 1, 0.5), nrow = N) else corr_binary(N, D, rho)
}

# ── One experiment (complete randomization, true effect = 0) ─────────────────
#   pass       : meets max |cor(T, X_j)| < r_max (strict; exact ties fail)
#   contrib    : realized prognostic contribution  sum_j beta_j * (mean X_j treated - mean X_j control),
#                the part of the observed contrast that comes from chance imbalance (outcome units; residual SD = 1)
#   reject     : unadjusted t-test rejects at 5%
#   reject_adj : the same test after adjusting for the measured characteristics
sim_once <- function(N, D, rho, betas, r_max = 0.10) {
  X    <- draw_X(N, D, rho)
  Tr   <- sample(rep(0:1, N / 2))
  cors <- apply(X, 2, function(x) if (sd(x) == 0) 0 else cor(x, Tr))
  contrib <- sum(betas * (colMeans(X[Tr == 1, , drop = FALSE]) - colMeans(X[Tr == 0, , drop = FALSE])))
  Y0   <- as.vector(X %*% betas) + rnorm(N)
  p_adj <- summary(lm(Y0 ~ Tr + X))$coefficients["Tr", 4]
  c(pass       = as.numeric(max(abs(cors)) < r_max - 1e-9),
    contrib    = contrib,
    reject     = as.numeric(t.test(Y0[Tr == 1], Y0[Tr == 0])$p.value < 0.05),
    reject_adj = as.numeric(p_adj < 0.05))
}

Top — meeting the balance criterion. With tightly clustered characteristics (Scenario A) the criterion is met most often, which is the helpful force at work: strong correlation collapses the number of effective checks. Scenario B behaves much like independent characteristics.

Middle — realized contribution of chance imbalance. In every scenario the contribution is centred on zero: complete randomization does not bias the estimator in expectation. But individual experiments deviate. At N = 200 the middle 90% of draws spans about ±0.45 outcome units in Scenario B, versus ±0.13 in Scenario A. This is the sense in which large, correlated, strongly prognostic characteristics make a single realized experiment noisier — and that variability is already part of the estimator’s sampling variability.

Bottom — false-positive rates. Across all experiments the ordinary t-test rejects about 5% of the time in every scenario and at every N (observed range 4.2%–5.3%). Conditioning on a post-randomization diagnostic is a different matter: among the experiments that fail the fixed 0.10 criterion, the unadjusted test rejects more often, most clearly in Scenario B at large N (24% at N = 800, where only 5% of experiments fail). Adjusting for the measured characteristics brings the conditional rate back to roughly the nominal level.

NoteWhat this does — and does not — show
  • It is not evidence that larger valid experiments are more dangerous. The overall error rate stays near 5% at every N. The conditional rate rises because a fixed cutoff of |r| = 0.10 corresponds to an ever more extreme standardized imbalance as N grows (the standard error of r shrinks like 1/√N). The experiments that still fail at large N are the extreme tail of the assignment distribution, and in this simulation the unadjusted test ignores information that the measured imbalance carries.
  • The practical lesson is to use measured prognostic covariates, not to certify balance with them. If you know which characteristics strongly predict Y, block on them in the design or adjust for them in the analysis (and pre-specify that choice). Adjustment uses the information in realized imbalance; a balance table alone does not.
  • None of this makes the p-value “invalid” because a diagnostic flagged imbalance. Validity of the randomization-based test is a statement about the assignment mechanism averaged over its possible draws. Conditional on a particular realized imbalance, the unadjusted test can be miscalibrated, which is why adjustment and blocking are useful.

The practical lesson: correlated characteristics are not uniformly good or bad for realized balance. The key question is what their β values look like. If correlated characteristics carry similar, modest effects, correlation genuinely helps by reducing the number of effective checks and keeps the contribution of any imbalance small. If they carry large, heterogeneous effects, a realized imbalance can move the estimate substantially — and that is invisible unless you look at the contribution, not just at whether the balance criterion was met.

This mirrors a core lesson from Module 1: a tight subscale structure looks efficient and reliable, but if the subscales carry separate predictive variance (discriminant invalidity), a problem in one cascades to all. The structural parallel is close.

Checking the Observables: Randomization Diagnostics

ImportantA fundamental limitation — you can only check what you measured

You can only discover realized imbalance on characteristics you thought to measure.

This is the direct experimental parallel to Module 1’s measurement problem. In Module 1, we showed that unmeasured sub-dimensions of Y produce a biased estimate of your latent construct — you cannot diagnose measurement failure on dimensions you never operationalized. The same logic applies here: if a potential confounder was never measured as a covariate, no balance table, no Love plot, and no omnibus test will catch it.

This constraint is not a limitation of statistics — it is a fundamental epistemological constraint. Its implication is that randomization checking is only as good as the covariate measurement you did before the study began. The tools from Module 1 — CFA to identify sub-dimensions of Y, pilot text analysis to map what’s in X and Y (see Part 4), discriminant validity checks — are not just measurement tools. They are pre-registration scaffolding that determines whether your eventual balance diagnostics have the construct coverage to be meaningful.

The practical lesson: Before collecting data, use the construct-mapping tools from Module 1 to enumerate every plausible antecedent of Y. Measure them all. Then, after randomization, describe balance on every one of them. The diagnostics below are only informative if you did that groundwork first.

You cannot observe characteristics you did not measure, but you can describe the ones you did. Balance tables, standardized mean differences, and omnibus tests describe the observed baseline differences in the realized sample, and they can help to investigate implementation problems — a broken allocation script, or non-random recruitment into conditions. They are not a certification procedure. In a properly randomized experiment, a significance test of a baseline difference tests a null hypothesis that is known to be true, so about 5% of such tests will “fail” by chance; standardized differences, read against how strongly each characteristic predicts Y, are more informative than p-values.

Here we simulate a study in which the assignment mechanism was flawed — participants who saw the study on an eco-forum were more likely to receive the eco-label version, so assignment depended on environmental values and income — and show how these diagnostics can reveal it.

Handling mixed covariate types

In practice, your covariates will rarely all be the same type. You may have binary indicators (gender, prior exposure), ordered categories (education level, income bracket), unordered categories (nationality, recruitment channel), and continuous measures (age, prior attitude score). The omnibus logistic regression approach handles all of these — you just need to encode each type correctly before passing them to glm:

# ── Step 1: Encode each covariate type correctly ──────────────────────────────
# Suppose your covariate data frame is called `covariates` with columns:
#   gender       : binary (0/1 or factor with 2 levels)
#   edu_level    : ordinal (e.g., 1=high school, 2=bachelor, 3=master, 4=PhD)
#   country      : nominal (unordered categories → MUST be factor)
#   age          : continuous
#   prior_att    : continuous (e.g., prior attitude score on 1–7 scale)

covariates$edu_level <- as.integer(covariates$edu_level)  # ordinal → numeric (linear trend)
covariates$country   <- factor(covariates$country)        # nominal → factor (K-1 dummies)
# age and prior_att stay as numeric — no conversion needed

# ── Step 2: Omnibus logistic regression ───────────────────────────────────────
# treatment must be a binary 0/1 variable
# glm automatically dummy-codes all factor variables
omnibus_fit  <- glm(treatment ~ ., data = covariates, family = binomial)
null_fit     <- glm(treatment ~ 1, data = covariates, family = binomial)

lrt <- anova(null_fit, omnibus_fit, test = "LRT")
cat(sprintf("Omnibus LRT p = %.4f\n", lrt$`Pr(>Chi)`[2]))

# Nagelkerke pseudo-R²
n_obs  <- nrow(covariates)
nag_r2 <- (1 - exp(2 * (logLik(null_fit) - logLik(omnibus_fit)) / n_obs)) /

  • Binary covariates: encode as factor — produces 1 dummy variable.
  • Ordinal covariates: encode as integer (assumes linear trend across levels). If the relationship might be non-linear, use factor() instead — this creates K-1 dummies but uses more degrees of freedom.

When You Can’t Check What You Can’t See: Diagnosing Latent Imbalance

The three diagnostics above — balance table, Love plot, omnibus test — are essential, but they share a fundamental limitation:

You can only check balance on dimensions you thought to measure.

If an unmeasured dimension of Y is correlated with treatment assignment, no balance table will catch it. And as the analysis in “From Exact Coverage to Approximate Orthogonality” showed, the broader your outcome construct, the more such unmeasured dimensions there are likely to be.

What can you do?

What you can do before data collection

1. Map the construct before running the study. Use Module 1’s toolkit — confirmatory factor analysis, convergent/discriminant validity checks, latent class analysis — on pilot data to identify all the major sub-dimensions of Y. Every dimension you identify is a confounder you can now measure and check balance on.

2. Blocked (stratified) randomization. If you know in advance that certain dimensions of Y are likely to be distributed unevenly in your sample, pre-stratify on them. Assign treatment within blocks defined by those dimensions. This guarantees balance on the dimensions you can observe — reducing the degrees of freedom for latent imbalance to exploit.

3. Collect more observations. As “From Exact Coverage to Approximate Orthogonality” showed, the probability of meeting a given simultaneous balance tolerance on all D characteristics grows with N. If your outcome is broad (large D), a sample size chosen for the treatment effect alone will often leave realized imbalance on prognostic characteristics larger than you would like.

What you can do after data collection

4. Adjust for measured prognostic covariates. If you measured characteristics that strongly predict Y, adjusting for them in the analysis (a pre-specified choice) uses the information in any realized imbalance on those characteristics. It does not help with characteristics you did not measure.

5. Open-ended text as a nomological net scanner — and an identification strategy.

As developed in detail in Part 4 (What’s in X? What’s in Y?), open-ended text responses from a small pilot are the most powerful tool for mapping the construct space before collecting main-study data. The nomological net scan reveals the exhaustive list of intermediary variables in the causal chain from X to Y. Every concept the text reveals is a mediator you should measure quantitatively — and a confounder you should check balance on.

The key insight from Part 4 bears repeating here: without the complete mediator list, the treatment coefficient in a regression is an uninterpretable mixture of all active pathways. With the list, you gain a structural identification strategy: decompose the total treatment effect into its component channels using the measured mediators.

The complete structural modeling demonstration — quantitative mediator scoring, parallel mediation, SEM with latent factors, and DGP recovery comparison — is developed step by step in Part 4 of this module, where it serves as the practical illustration of how to handle exclusion restriction violations once the nomological net has been mapped.
