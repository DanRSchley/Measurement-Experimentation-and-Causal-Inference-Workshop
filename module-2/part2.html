Part 2: What Does “Random Assignment” Actually Require?

What Does Randomization Actually Guarantee?

We tend to talk about randomization as if it were a button. Click “Randomize” in Qualtrics, split people into treatment and control, move on, and act as if the two groups are now basically the same. But what did that button actually promise?

Here is the one-line version of this whole page:

Randomization is a procedure. Exchangeability is what a valid procedure gives you. Balance is what you actually see in the one experiment you ran.

Let’s unpack that in plain terms. Every person has two “what-if” outcomes: what their score would be if they got the treatment, and what it would be if they didn’t. Researchers call these potential outcomes. (We won’t use them properly until “The Fundamental Problem of Causal Inference” (Module 3, Part 1), so for now just think “the what-ifs.”) Random assignment means nobody’s what-ifs get to decide which group they land in. Picture re-running the assignment over and over: on average, treatment and control would line up, because the assignment never peeked at anyone’s what-ifs. That is exchangeability: as far as the what-ifs go, the two groups are swappable.

(For the notation fans, that is the statement \(\bigl(Y^{(0)}, Y^{(1)}\bigr) \perp\!\!\!\perp T\): the what-ifs are independent of who got the treatment.)

Notice the catch. “Swappable on average across many possible assignments” is not the same as “identical in the one assignment you actually got.” Any single draw can put more eco-minded, price-sensitive, or high-income people into one group. And this is true whether you have 20 participants or 2,000: the guarantee comes from how people were assigned, not from how many there are.

So when two groups look different, there are two very different explanations:

  1. The assignment was flawed. Something let participants’ what-ifs influence who got which condition (self-selection, a predictable allocation sequence, a bug). That can undermine causal identification.
  2. The assignment was fine, and the groups differ by luck. That adds random variation to a valid estimate. It does not invalidate randomization.
Question What answers it?
Was assignment genuinely random? How the assignment was actually done, which you document and check
Do the groups we got look similar? A plain description of their baseline characteristics
How much does a difference matter? How strongly those characteristics are tied to the outcome

This echoes Module 1: something you can see (a balance table) is not the whole story about the process behind it. But the assignment procedure isn’t a hidden construct you measure with error. It’s something you can and should verify directly.

Shuffling Cards: The Mechanism Versus the Hand You Receive

Consider how random assignment works when shuffling a deck of cards.

Was the deck shuffled properly? Start with a deck sorted by suit and shuffle it once, badly. A lot of the original order survives, so the shuffle didn’t get you anywhere near a random deck. A sloppy shuffle is the analogue of a flawed assignment. The question is how the order was produced, not whether one particular arrangement looks random.

What hands did we happen to get? Now deal a properly shuffled deck into two hands of 26. On average each hand gets two aces and 13 red cards. But with a perfectly valid shuffle, one hand can easily get three aces while the other gets one, or even all four.

Both hands have 26 cards and 13 red cards, so they’re perfectly balanced on those two things. But hand 1 holds 3 aces and hand 2 holds 1. With a valid shuffle, the aces split evenly 2–2 only 39% of the time, and all four end up in one hand 11% of the time. Lopsided hands don’t mean the shuffle was broken.

  • Good shuffle, uneven hands: valid mechanism, imbalance by luck.
  • Bad shuffle, even-looking hands: shaky mechanism, balance that looks reassuring.

Notice, too, that being balanced on one thing (red cards) tells you nothing about another (aces). Hold onto that; we’ll come back to it.

Technical extension: how many riffle shuffles are enough?

For a real shuffle, the question “is it random yet?” has an exact answer (Bayer & Diaconis, 1992). The figure shows how far the order of a deck is from perfectly random after k riffle shuffles. A score of 0 means every ordering is equally likely; a score near 1 means the shuffle leaves the deck stuck in a small corner of all possible orders.

▶ Exact total variation distance for GSR riffle shuffles (code) 
# Eulerian counts E(n, r) for r = 1..n: the number of permutations of n cards with exactly
# r rising sequences. Recurrence: E(1,1) = 1; E(n,r) = r * E(n-1,r) + (n-r+1) * E(n-1,r-1),
# with values outside 1..n-1 equal to zero. The counts for each n sum to n!.
eulerian_counts <- function(n) {
  E <- 1
  if (n > 1) for (m in 2:n) {
    r     <- seq_len(m)
    prev  <- c(E, rep(0, m - length(E)))                    # E(m-1, r)
    prev1 <- c(0, E, rep(0, m - 1 - length(E)))             # E(m-1, r-1)
    E <- r * prev + (m - r + 1) * prev1
  }
  E
}

# Exact total variation distance from the uniform distribution on the n! orderings after k GSR
# riffle shuffles (Bayer & Diaconis, 1992). Probabilities are computed on the log scale.
riffle_tv <- function(n, k) {
  a    <- 2^k
  r    <- seq_len(n)
  P_r  <- exp(lchoose(a + n - r, n) - n * log(a))           # probability of ONE permutation with r rising sequences
  0.5 * sum(eulerian_counts(n) * abs(P_r - exp(-lgamma(n + 1))))
}

deck_sizes <- c(5, 10, 20, 52)
tv_df <- expand.grid(cards = deck_sizes, shuffles = 0:12) |>
  mutate(tv = mapply(riffle_tv, cards, shuffles),
         cards_f = factor(cards))

One riffle shuffle leaves even a 5-card deck far from random (distance 0.775). For 52 cards the distance is about 0.334 after seven shuffles, 0.167 after eight, and 0.085 after nine. The familiar “seven shuffles” rule marks the point where things start to settle down fast, not the point of perfect randomness, and the dashed 0.10 line is just an illustrative benchmark.

For the mathematically inclined: with \(a = 2^k\), a permutation of n cards with r rising sequences has probability \(\binom{a+n-r}{n}/a^{n}\) after k riffle shuffles, and the distance is \(\tfrac12\sum_{r=1}^{n} E(n,r)\,\bigl|P(n,k,r) - 1/n!\bigr|\), where \(E(n,r)\) counts permutations with r rising sequences. This is a property of the whole distribution of decks a shuffle can produce. You can’t read it off one deck, and a quick check like “is the deck still in its original order?” can look fine while the distribution is still far from random.

The point is that the more cards there are in the deck, the more times you need to press the “randomize” button to get something that looks random. In the next section, we discuss how this relates to the dependent variables we aim to study in our research.

From Playing Cards to Psychological Constructs

Now let’s swap cards for people. Each participant has background characteristics that affect the outcome, the way each card has a colour and a rank. Below are two made-up scenarios. They aren’t claims that narrow outcomes always have fewer causes than broad psychological ones. They’re there to show what changes when a few characteristics matter versus many. And importantly, not all characteristics matter equally: some move the outcome a lot, some barely at all. The “effect” columns give a hypothetical size, in standard deviations of the outcome, so we can come back to who matters most.

Does that mean every experiment with 80 participants breaks the rules of an experiment, just because it can’t represent every profile? No. What matters is how those 80 participants end up distributed across the profiles, that is, the composition of the groups. This is worth taking seriously, because we rarely care only about the people in front of us. We study average treatment effects because we want to say something that generalizes. If chance imbalance on characteristics that matter is producing part of the difference we observe, that gets in the way of the effect we are actually after. Whether it does depends on how the groups are composed, and that is what the next section is about.

From Exact Coverage to Approximate Orthogonality

We can’t cover every profile, so the goal becomes more modest: we want who got treated to be roughly unrelated to the characteristics that drive the outcome. (The technical name for “roughly unrelated” is approximately orthogonal.) Let’s see what that looks like.

Step A: make the imbalance concrete

▶ Correlation between treatment and a binary characteristic, from group counts 
# Correlation between treatment (exactly m treated, m control) and a binary characteristic, given
# that `a` treated and `b` control participants have X = 1. Constant characteristics (p = 0 or 1)
# have no group difference, so r = 0 rather than NaN.
treat_cov_cor <- function(a, b, m) {
  p1 <- a / m; p0 <- b / m; p <- (a + b) / (2 * m)
  ifelse(p == 0 | p == 1, 0, (p1 - p0) / (2 * sqrt(p * (1 - p))))
}
treat_cov_cor_ex <- treat_cov_cor(22, 18, 40)      # 55% vs. 45% high among 40 + 40 participants

Take 80 participants, 40 in each condition, and say exactly 40 of them have high environmental concern. Perfect balance would put 20 high-concern people in each group (50% vs. 50%). One perfectly valid assignment might put 22 in treatment and 18 in control: 55% vs. 45%. That’s a ten-percentage-point gap from moving two people. For a characteristic that half the sample has, with equal-sized groups, the correlation between treatment and the characteristic equals the gap in proportions, so here the correlation is r = 0.10.

We’ll call \(r_{\max}\) the biggest treatment-characteristic correlation we’re willing to put up with across the characteristics we check. Now give people all ten characteristics from the table above. Here is one valid assignment of 80 people (labels show each characteristic’s hypothetical effect on the outcome):

Step B: more characteristics make strict balance less likely

Let’s make that exact under a deliberately simple setup: equal-sized groups, D characteristics that are independent of one another, each “high” for half the sample, a fixed tolerance of \(|r| < 0.10\), and valid randomization in every experiment.

▶ Exact probability of meeting the marginal-balance criterion (code) 
# q(N): probability that ONE independent Bernoulli(0.5) characteristic has |cor(T, X)| < thresh
# under complete randomization with N/2 per arm. The inequality is strict; exact ties
# (|r| = thresh) count as failing, with a tiny tolerance to guard against floating-point error.
q_balance <- function(N, thresh = 0.10) {
  stopifnot(N %% 2 == 0)
  m  <- N / 2
  pm <- dbinom(0:m, m, 0.5)                          # P(a) and P(b): Binomial(N/2, 0.5)
  g  <- expand.grid(a = 0:m, b = 0:m)
  r  <- treat_cov_cor(g$a, g$b, m)
  sum(pm[g$a + 1] * pm[g$b + 1] * (abs(r) < thresh - 1e-9))
}

# P(pass on all D independent characteristics) = q(N)^D
p_balance_all <- function(N, D, thresh = 0.10) q_balance(N, thresh)^D

bal_tab <- expand.grid(N = c(80, 200, 400), D = c(1, 2, 5, 10)) |>
  mutate(p = mapply(p_balance_all, N, D))

At 80 participants the chance of meeting the criterion on every characteristic is 32.0% for two characteristics and 0.3% for ten. More participants make strict balance easier; more characteristics make it harder. These aren’t the probabilities that randomization “worked” or that exchangeability holds: the assignment is valid throughout. They also rest on the simple setup above, so they aren’t universal sample-size rules. (The math behind them: for each possible pair of counts of “high” people in the two groups we work out the correlation and its probability, add up the pairs with \(|r| < 0.10\), and raise the one-characteristic answer to the power D.)

This is the playing-card lesson in new clothes. As the deck gets bigger, you need more shuffles before it looks random. In the same way, as the number of meaningful characteristics grows from D = 2 to D = 10, you need more participants to have a reasonable chance of being balanced on all of them. It reinforces the point that pressing a randomization button doesn’t guarantee what we would colloquially call a nicely randomized set of observations.

When Does Imbalance Actually Matter?

Say the treated group ended up with more environmentally concerned people. Does that matter? Only as much as environmental concern drives the outcome you care about. A simple way to see it: suppose the outcome is the treatment effect \(\tau\) plus a contribution from some characteristic \(C\) plus noise,

\[Y = \tau T + \beta\,C + \varepsilon .\]

Then the difference you observe between the groups is

\[\bar Y_T - \bar Y_C = \tau + \beta\,(\bar C_T - \bar C_C) + (\bar\varepsilon_T - \bar\varepsilon_C).\]

In words: the real treatment effect, plus how strongly \(C\) predicts the outcome times how much \(C\) differs between the groups, plus leftover noise. That middle piece is what imbalance costs you.

Take environmental values from our table (effect 0.80 SD). If the groups differ on it by 0.10, chance imbalance adds 0.08 SD to what you observe. Take age (effect 0.05 SD) with the same 0.10 gap, and it adds just 0.005 SD. What matters is how big the gap is and how much the characteristic drives the outcome.

Chance imbalance versus omitted-variable bias

The algebra above is the same algebra as omitted-variable bias in Module 1, Part 2, but what it means depends on how the groups came to differ.

  • A flawed or observational assignment. Say the eco-label version of a study was advertised on an environmental forum and the control version wasn’t. Now environmental values are tied to who got treated, so leaving them out creates systematic bias. With a 0.23 gap in the share of environmentally minded participants and an effect of $1.20 on WTP, that’s about $0.28 baked into the comparison.
  • A valid randomization, but an unlucky draw. The assignment was random, yet we still ended up with imbalance. Across all possible assignments that imbalance averages out to zero, but we only ran one of them.

That second case leads to a distinction worth knowing. The sample average treatment effect is the average effect for the people actually in your study. The average treatment effect is what we usually want when we generalize beyond them. An unlucky draw can push the first away from the second.

It also shows what randomization really buys us. Because assignment is random, characteristics related to the outcome (say, prior green behavior) are unrelated to who got treated, so we can leave them out of our analysis rather than controlling for them. That is the dream: just compare the groups, and don’t measure anything else in the data-generating process. But the dream rests on those characteristics being balanced in our sample. When they aren’t, they can distort the estimated treatment effect in the same way an omitted variable would, and we then need to have measured them so that we can control for them.

This is where the complexity of the context comes in. For visual attention there are only a few profiles to worry about. For sustainable purchase intention the set of possible profiles is vastly larger, so we need much larger samples to get close to approximate orthogonality. If we can’t get samples that large, we still need to measure the characteristics that drive the outcome, so that we can check balance and correct for imbalance. Randomization doesn’t remove ordinary sampling variability, differences in who responds to treatment, or measurement error either.

What Makes Balance Harder, or More Consequential?

Some of these change how likely it is that you meet a balance target. Others change what an imbalance does to your estimate. Measuring more covariates doesn’t by itself make a randomized estimate less precise. What matters is how much outcome variation they explain.

Balanced on each thing, but not on the combinations

Here is a trap that single-variable balance tables can’t catch. Suppose we’re studying how much people will pay for eco-friendly coffee, and two characteristics matter: environmental concern and price sensitivity. Say people pay a lot more only when they are both concerned about the environment and not price sensitive.

Both groups are 50% high on environmental concern and 50% high on price sensitivity, so a balance table checking one characteristic at a time shows perfect balance. But look at the combinations. 15 of the 40 treated people are concerned and not price sensitive, compared with 5 of the 40 controls. Because that combination is exactly where the WTP is high, the treated group’s untreated WTP is higher by about $0.50 even though both groups look balanced on each characteristic.

And it doesn’t stop at that one combination. Balance on the overall distribution of each characteristic doesn’t guarantee balance within other slices of the data. Among the environmentally concerned participants, 25% of the treated group is price sensitive versus 75% of controls. That matters whenever the slice is meaningful: your effect of interest might live in that slice, or your sample might be split that way in a later analysis. The more characteristics you have, the more slices there are in which the groups can quietly differ.

This is a deliberately extreme, constructed example. A valid randomization can produce a pattern like this, though it would be unusual. The point is what single-variable tables can’t see.

When the characteristics are correlated

Everything so far has assumed the characteristics are independent of each other. In the real world they aren’t: people who care about the environment also tend to care about health, social norms, and so on. So what happens when characteristics travel together?

It can go either way, and which way depends on how their effects on the outcome line up. The simulation below uses ten characteristics that are either independent or correlated, complete randomization, and a fixed effect of 0.3 SD per characteristic, and it asks two questions: how often is a strict balance rule met, and how big is the swing that chance imbalance adds to the estimate?

▶ Simulate: correlated characteristics, same-direction vs. opposite-direction effects (≈30 s) 
corr_binary <- function(N, D, rho) {
  Sigma <- matrix(rho, D, D); diag(Sigma) <- 1
  z <- matrix(rnorm(N * D), nrow = N) %*% chol(Sigma)
  (z > 0L) * 1L
}
draw_X <- function(N, D, rho) if (rho == 0) matrix(rbinom(N * D, 1, 0.5), nrow = N) else corr_binary(N, D, rho)

D_cs <- 10
beta_cs <- 0.3                                   # effect of each characteristic on Y (SD units)
alt_sign <- rep(c(1, -1), length.out = D_cs)     # signs of effects when they alternate
rho_vals <- c(0, 0.35, 0.75)
N_vals_cs <- c(50, 100, 200, 400, 800)

one_exp <- function(N, rho) {
  X <- draw_X(N, D_cs, rho); Tr <- sample(rep(0:1, N / 2))
  cors <- apply(X, 2, function(x) if (sd(x) == 0) 0 else cor(x, Tr))
  d <- colMeans(X[Tr == 1, , drop = FALSE]) - colMeans(X[Tr == 0, , drop = FALSE])     # realized group differences
  c(pass = as.numeric(max(abs(cors)) < 0.10 - 1e-9), same = sum(d), alt = sum(alt_sign * d))
}

  • Panel A: the more the characteristics move together, the more often a strict balance rule gets met. There are simply fewer separate things to balance.
  • Panel B: what correlation does to the consequences depends on the effects. If correlated characteristics all push the outcome in the same direction, an imbalance on one comes with imbalances on the others and the contributions add up: the swing here grows from 0.07 to 0.16 SD. If they push in opposite directions, the contributions cancel and the swing shrinks (from 0.07 to 0.05 SD).

So correlation among characteristics can greatly worsen things or greatly improve them, and it’s an empirical question which one you’re in. The practical takeaway is the one from the previous section: random assignment having balanced the characteristics that sit closest to the data-generating process, the ones that really drive the outcome, is what protects you. Balance on characteristics that barely matter is not worth much, and imbalance on the ones that matter is the thing to worry about.

Balance Versus Power: Two Different Sample-Size Questions

One last comparison, because it clears up a lot of confusion. “How many participants do I need?” can mean two very different things:

  • Power: enough participants to reliably detect a treatment effect.
  • Balance: enough participants that the groups look similar on a strict criterion across many characteristics.

With an effect of 0.4 SD, you reach 80% power at about N = 200. Reaching an 80% chance of strict balance on ten characteristics takes about N = 560. The sample you need to find a difference is much smaller than the sample you’d need to make the groups look alike on everything, and that’s fine. A valid randomized experiment doesn’t need strict balance to give you an unbiased estimate, and the standard error already accounts for the luck of the draw. Balance matters for the characteristics that drive the outcome, and for those you have better tools than sample size: blocking, rerandomization, and adjustment.

What Should Researchers Actually Do?

Three different problems can undermine a randomized comparison:

  1. A flawed assignment procedure.
  2. Chance imbalance after a valid assignment.
  3. Losing comparability after assignment: people dropping out, being excluded, switching treatment, or being selected in some other way. This can ruin an analysis even when the randomization was done perfectly (Module 2, Part 3).

Before the experiment

  • Document and protect the assignment procedure. Write down the unit you randomize, the allocation ratio, how it’s implemented, who can see it, and how you’ll handle non-compliance and dropout.
  • Pick a manageable set of characteristics that really predict the outcome. Use theory, past research, and pilot data to find the ones that drive your outcome most. The more complex the outcome, the more of them there are, and the larger the sample you need to be balanced on them. If you can’t get a sample that large, measure them anyway so that you can check balance and correct for imbalance. You don’t need to measure everything that might possibly matter, just the characteristics that matter most.
  • Consider blocking. If you already know a few strong predictors, randomize within groups defined by them (see Module 2, Part 5). That improves balance on those, not on everything else.
  • Consider rerandomization. You can decide in advance what counts as acceptable balance and redraw the assignment until it’s met (Morgan & Rubin, 2012). That’s a design choice made up front, not fishing, and your analysis should reflect the restriction.

After the experiment

  • Verify the procedure. Check assignment logs, deviations from the plan, group sizes, and any sign that assignment wasn’t really random.
  • Describe the balance you got. Report standardized differences for characteristics measured before treatment, and focus on how big the differences are and how much those characteristics predict the outcome, rather than pass/fail cutoffs. Things that respond to the manipulation (perceived quality, emotional reactions) aren’t baseline characteristics.
  • Watch for post-randomization selection. Looking only at people who received the treatment, dropping some participants, or analyzing only completers can break a randomized comparison if who gets dropped is related to treatment and outcomes. Keep an intention-to-treat analysis and look carefully at missing data and non-compliance.
  • Correct for imbalance that matters. If the groups ended up different on characteristics that strongly predict the outcome, control for them, ideally in an analysis you planned in advance. Left alone, that imbalance can distort your estimate the way an omitted variable would, and it separates the effect in your sample from the effect you want to generalize. Adjustment won’t fix imbalance on things you didn’t measure or repair a flawed assignment, and don’t adjust for mediators or other post-treatment variables when you want the total effect.

The check flags environmental values and income, which is exactly how we built in the flaw. In a properly randomized experiment, baseline significance tests have a null hypothesis that is true by design, so about 5% of them will flag by chance. Treat them as a way to look for implementation slip-ups, not as a certificate. Read the standardized differences against how strongly each characteristic predicts the outcome.

When you can’t check what you can’t see

A balance table only covers what you measured. Pilot data and open-ended responses (see Part 4) can help you list candidate characteristics measured before treatment that you could block on or adjust for. Keep those separate from mediators, which treatment can change: don’t balance-check or adjust for mediators when you want the total effect. A mediator list becomes relevant when you want to study pathways, and even then measuring mediators doesn’t establish their causal effects (Module 3, Part 4).

Researcher Checklist: Exchangeability

NoteKey questions about your randomization

Randomization doesn’t guarantee identical groups. It guarantees a valid assignment procedure, one where differences between the groups aren’t systematically driven by what participants’ outcomes would have been. What’s left in any one experiment is chance variation. The job of good design and analysis is to understand and manage that variation, not to pretend it isn’t there.
