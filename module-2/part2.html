Part 2: What Does “Random Assignment” Actually Require?

What Does Randomization Actually Guarantee?

Randomization is often treated as something we do rather than something we need to understand. We click “Randomize” in Qualtrics, assign participants to treatment and control, and proceed as though the groups are equivalent. But what exactly did that button guarantee?

Randomization is a procedure. Exchangeability is a property established by a valid assignment mechanism. Realized balance describes what happened in one particular assignment.

If assignment is genuinely random, participants’ potential outcomes do not determine which condition they receive. Across the possible assignments, treatment and control are comparable by design. But any one assignment can still put more high-value, price-sensitive, or environmentally concerned people in one condition than the other.

Formally, exchangeability says that potential outcomes are independent of treatment:

\[\bigl(Y^{(0)}, Y^{(1)}\bigr) \perp\!\!\!\perp T .\]

That independence is established by a correctly implemented assignment mechanism, over its possible realizations. It holds by design, in small samples as well as large ones, and it does not require the characteristics of the groups to be identical in the one assignment you observe.

Two different things can make observed groups differ, with different consequences:

  1. A flawed assignment. Participants were not assigned independently of their potential outcomes (self-selection, a predictable allocation sequence, a bug). This can undermine causal identification.
  2. A valid assignment with chance differences. The groups differ by luck on characteristics that matter for the outcome. This adds random variation to a valid randomized estimate. It does not invalidate randomization.
Question What establishes it?
Was assignment genuinely random? The documented and correctly implemented assignment mechanism
Are the realized groups similar? Descriptive comparisons of their baseline characteristics
How much does a difference matter? The relationship between baseline characteristics and potential outcomes

This echoes Module 1: what you can observe (a balance table) is not complete evidence about the process that produced it. But the assignment mechanism is not a latent construct measured with error; it is something you document and verify.

A balance table describes the groups we received. It does not certify the assignment mechanism.

Shuffling Cards: The Mechanism Versus the Hand You Receive

Playing cards separate the two ideas cleanly.

Was the deck shuffled properly? Start with a deck arranged by suit. After one limited shuffle, a lot of that structure survives, so the shuffle did not produce anything close to a uniformly random order. A flawed shuffling procedure is the analogue of a flawed assignment mechanism. What matters is how the order was generated, not whether one particular arrangement happens to look random.

What hands did we happen to receive? Now deal a properly shuffled deck into two hands of 26. On average each hand gets two aces and 13 red cards. But under a perfectly valid shuffle, one hand can get three aces and the other one, or even all four.

Both hands have 26 cards and 13 red cards: exactly balanced on those two features. But hand 1 has 3 aces and hand 2 has 1. Under a valid shuffle, the aces split exactly 2–2 only 39% of the time, and all four land in one hand 11% of the time. Unequal hands do not mean the shuffle was defective.

  • Proper shuffle, uneven hands: valid mechanism, realized imbalance.
  • Poor shuffle, apparently even hands: questionable mechanism, good-looking realized balance.

And balance on one feature (red cards) says nothing about another (aces). A characteristic can be perfectly balanced without the groups being equivalent on everything that matters. We return to this under marginal versus joint balance.

NoteTechnical extension: How many riffle shuffles are enough?

For a hand shuffle the question has an exact answer (Bayer & Diaconis, 1992). Let \(a = 2^k\). Under the Gilbert–Shannon–Reeds (GSR) model, any permutation of n cards with r rising sequences has probability \(P(n,k,r)=\binom{a+n-r}{n}/a^{n}\) after k riffle shuffles. With \(E(n,r)\) the Eulerian number of permutations having exactly r rising sequences, the exact total variation (TV) distance from the uniform distribution on all n! orderings is

\[\mathrm{TV}(n,k) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{r=1}^{n} E(n,r)\,\left|P(n,k,r) - \frac{1}{n!}\right|.\]

TV = 0 means every ordering is equally likely; TV near 1 means a distribution concentrated on a small fraction of orderings. TV is a property of the distribution the procedure generates; it cannot be recovered from one observed deck, and a statistic such as correlation with the original order can look random while TV is still large.

▶ Exact total variation distance for GSR riffle shuffles (code) 
# Eulerian counts E(n, r) for r = 1..n: the number of permutations of n cards with exactly
# r rising sequences. Recurrence: E(1,1) = 1; E(n,r) = r * E(n-1,r) + (n-r+1) * E(n-1,r-1),
# with values outside 1..n-1 equal to zero. The counts for each n sum to n!.
eulerian_counts <- function(n) {
  E <- 1
  if (n > 1) for (m in 2:n) {
    r     <- seq_len(m)
    prev  <- c(E, rep(0, m - length(E)))                    # E(m-1, r)
    prev1 <- c(0, E, rep(0, m - 1 - length(E)))             # E(m-1, r-1)
    E <- r * prev + (m - r + 1) * prev1
  }
  E
}

# Exact total variation distance from the uniform distribution on the n! orderings after k GSR
# riffle shuffles (Bayer & Diaconis, 1992). Probabilities are computed on the log scale.
riffle_tv <- function(n, k) {
  a    <- 2^k
  r    <- seq_len(n)
  P_r  <- exp(lchoose(a + n - r, n) - n * log(a))           # probability of ONE permutation with r rising sequences
  0.5 * sum(eulerian_counts(n) * abs(P_r - exp(-lgamma(n + 1))))
}

deck_sizes <- c(5, 10, 20, 52)

One riffle shuffle leaves even a 5-card deck far from uniform (TV = 0.775). Seven shuffles of 52 cards give TV ≈ 0.334, eight 0.167, nine 0.085: the familiar “seven shuffles” marks the rapid-mixing transition, not perfect uniformity. The dashed 0.10 line is an illustrative benchmark.

Do not take the analogy literally. A correct random assignment algorithm draws the assignment from its intended distribution in one step (sample() draws a uniformly random subset directly). It does not need to be run repeatedly to become valid.

From Playing Cards to Psychological Constructs

Now translate the cards into experiments. Each participant has baseline characteristics that affect the outcome, the way each card has a colour and a rank. Below are two stylized data-generating scenarios. They are not claims that narrow outcomes inherently have fewer determinants than broad psychological constructs. What matters is the number of pre-treatment characteristics that affect potential outcomes, not the number of dimensions of a scale.

Suppose fixation time on an eco-label depends strongly on just two binary baseline characteristics: viewing distance (near vs. far) and display luminance (high vs. low). These create four possible profiles.

Does this mean 80 participants cannot validly estimate the effect of treatment on sustainable purchase intention? No. For the average treatment effect in the randomized sample, we do not need every possible profile represented in both groups. Random assignment identifies the average effect through the assignment mechanism, not through exhaustive matching of cells. Exact coverage matters when you want separate estimates for every profile, fine-grained effect heterogeneity, or claims about populations the sample does not represent.

From Exact Coverage to Approximate Orthogonality

Step A: make the imbalance concrete

▶ Correlation between treatment and a binary characteristic, from group counts 
# Correlation between treatment (exactly m treated, m control) and a binary characteristic, given
# that `a` treated and `b` control participants have X = 1. Constant characteristics (p = 0 or 1)
# have no group difference, so r = 0 rather than NaN.
treat_cov_cor <- function(a, b, m) {
  p1 <- a / m; p0 <- b / m; p <- (a + b) / (2 * m)
  ifelse(p == 0 | p == 1, 0, (p1 - p0) / (2 * sqrt(p * (1 - p))))
}
treat_cov_cor_ex <- treat_cov_cor(22, 18, 40)      # 55% vs. 45% high among 40 + 40 participants

Take 80 participants, 40 in each condition, and exactly 40 with high environmental concern. Perfect balance would put 20 in each condition (50% vs. 50%). One valid random assignment might instead give 22 in treatment and 18 in control: 55% vs. 45%, a ten-percentage-point difference produced by moving two people. For a characteristic with prevalence 0.5 and equal-sized groups, the correlation between treatment and the characteristic equals the difference in proportions, so here r = 0.10.

Call \(r_{\max}\) the largest realized absolute correlation we are willing to tolerate across the characteristics we examine. With ten characteristics instead of one, here is one valid assignment of 80 people:

Step B: more characteristics make strict simultaneous balance less likely

Make this exact under a deliberately simple setup: equal-sized arms, D independent binary characteristics each high with probability 0.5, a fixed tolerance \(|r| < 0.10\), and valid randomization in every experiment.

NoteTechnical extension: the exact probability calculation

With N/2 participants per arm, the number with X = 1 in each arm is Binomial(N/2, 0.5). For every pair of counts we compute the treatment–characteristic correlation \(r=(p_1-p_0)/\bigl(2\sqrt{p(1-p)}\bigr)\) (with pooled proportion p; constant characteristics count as balanced) and its probability. Summing the probabilities with |r| < 0.10 gives q(N) for one characteristic; independence gives q(N)D for all D. Exact ties at |r| = 0.10 count as failing.

▶ Exact probability of meeting the marginal-balance criterion (code) 
# q(N): probability that ONE independent Bernoulli(0.5) characteristic has |cor(T, X)| < thresh
# under complete randomization with N/2 per arm. The inequality is strict; exact ties
# (|r| = thresh) count as failing, with a tiny tolerance to guard against floating-point error.
q_balance <- function(N, thresh = 0.10) {
  stopifnot(N %% 2 == 0)
  m  <- N / 2
  pm <- dbinom(0:m, m, 0.5)                          # P(a) and P(b): Binomial(N/2, 0.5)
  g  <- expand.grid(a = 0:m, b = 0:m)
  r  <- treat_cov_cor(g$a, g$b, m)
  sum(pm[g$a + 1] * pm[g$b + 1] * (abs(r) < thresh - 1e-9))
}

# P(pass on all D independent characteristics) = q(N)^D
p_balance_all <- function(N, D, thresh = 0.10) q_balance(N, thresh)^D

bal_tab <- expand.grid(N = c(80, 200, 400), D = c(1, 2, 5, 10)) |>
  mutate(p = mapply(p_balance_all, N, D))

These probabilities are not the probability that randomization is valid or that exchangeability holds: the assignment mechanism is valid throughout. They also depend on the stated assumptions, so they are not universal sample-size requirements.

Step C: approximate orthogonality

Approximate orthogonality means small realized associations between treatment assignment and baseline characteristics. Valid randomization makes those associations zero in expectation, not in every realization. In this setup their typical size shrinks in proportion to \(1/\sqrt{N}\). That is the asymptotic fact that matters here: realized differences in many baseline characteristics tend to shrink as N grows. Exchangeability itself does not become “more valid” with N; it holds by design at any N.

A conservative planning approximation (a Bonferroni argument over the D characteristics) gives the sample size at which a valid randomization will meet a chosen tolerance on every characteristic with probability at least \(1-\alpha\):

\[N \gtrsim \left(\frac{z_{1-\alpha/(2D)}}{r_{\max}}\right)^{2}.\]

Here \(r_{\max}\) is the tolerance, D the number of characteristics, and \(z\) the standard normal quantile. This is a planning approximation for meeting a balance criterion, not a minimum sample size for valid randomized inference.

▶ Approximate N for a simultaneous balance tolerance (code) 
required_N <- function(D, r_thresh = 0.10, alpha = 0.05) {
  z_crit <- qnorm(1 - alpha / (2 * D))
  ceiling((z_crit / r_thresh)^2)
}

For ten characteristics and \(r_{\max} = 0.10\) the approximation gives N ≈ 788, which is conservative: the exact calculation gives 59% at N = 400 and 95% at N = 800.

When Does Realized Imbalance Actually Matter?

Suppose the treated group contains more environmentally concerned participants. Does it matter? Only to the extent that environmental concern predicts the outcome we are studying. In a simple additive model with a constant treatment effect \(\tau\),

\[Y = \tau T + \beta\,C + \varepsilon,\]

the observed difference in group means is

\[\bar Y_T - \bar Y_C = \tau + \beta\,(\bar C_T - \bar C_C) + (\bar\varepsilon_T - \bar\varepsilon_C).\]

The first term is the treatment effect. The second is the contribution of realized imbalance: how strongly C predicts Y times how much C differs between the groups. The third is remaining individual differences and noise.

Suppose the groups differ on environmental concern by 0.10 and environmental concern predicts an outcome difference of 0.80 SD: chance imbalance contributes 0.08 SD to the observed contrast. An equally imbalanced characteristic that predicts only 0.05 SD contributes 0.005 SD. An imbalance matters through its relationship with the outcome, not just its size.

Chance imbalance versus omitted-variable bias

The algebra above is the algebra of omitted-variable bias in Module 1, Part 2, but its status depends on how the groups came to differ.

  • Observational or flawed assignment. If assignment depends on a prognostic characteristic (say, the eco-label version was advertised on an environmental forum and the control version was not), the expected difference in C is not zero and omitting it creates systematic confounding bias. With a 0.23 difference in the share of high-environmental-values participants and an effect of $1.20 on WTP, the contribution is about $0.28.
  • Valid randomization. A realized difference in C is a chance deviation with expectation zero over the assignment distribution. It adds uncertainty, not systematic bias. It is not a new kind of bias in an otherwise valid randomization.

Good randomization also does not eliminate ordinary sampling variability, treatment-effect heterogeneity, or measurement error.

What Makes Realized Balance More Difficult or More Consequential?

Factor Effect
Smaller N Larger chance fluctuations in group composition
More independently varying characteristics Harder to satisfy strict simultaneous balance on all of them
Stronger prognostic relationships A given imbalance has larger consequences for the observed outcome
Correlated characteristics Fewer effectively independent balance checks, so a balance rule is met more often; consequences still depend on the characteristics’ combined predictive strength
Interactions and nonlinearities Marginal balance may conceal differences between joint profiles
Unequal allocation or clustering Changes effective information and the variability of realized comparisons

These affect different things: some change the probability of meeting a balance criterion, others change what an imbalance does to the outcome. Recording more covariates does not by itself make a randomized estimate less precise; what matters is how much outcome variation they explain.

Correlated characteristics

Correlation among characteristics and the strength with which they predict Y are different things. To keep them apart, the simulation below changes one thing at a time. Characteristics are ten independent or correlated binary variables, assignment is complete randomization, and the total prognostic strength (the SD of the part of Y they explain) is held fixed at 0.3 (weak) or 0.9 (strong) whatever the correlation.

What Should Researchers Actually Do?

Three different problems can undermine a randomized comparison:

  1. A flawed assignment mechanism.
  2. Chance imbalance after valid assignment.
  3. Loss of comparability after assignment: attrition, exclusions, treatment switching, or other selection. This can undermine an analysis even when the randomization was perfectly implemented (Module 2, Part 3).

Before the experiment

  • Document and protect the assignment mechanism. Specify the randomization unit, allocation ratio, implementation, concealment where relevant, and the planned handling of noncompliance and attrition.
  • Identify a manageable set of prognostic baseline characteristics. Use theory, earlier research and pilot data to find characteristics likely to explain meaningful variation in potential outcomes. You do not need to measure every plausible antecedent of Y: identifying an intention-to-treat effect under valid random assignment does not require it.
  • Consider blocking or stratification. If important prognostic characteristics are known before assignment, randomize within blocks. Blocking improves balance on the blocking structure; it does not guarantee balance on every other characteristic (see Module 2, Part 5).
  • Consider rerandomization. You can prespecify an acceptable-balance criterion and redraw assignments until it is met (Morgan & Rubin, 2012). This is a design choice made in advance, not post hoc manipulation, and the analysis should account for the restricted assignment mechanism.

After the experiment

  • Verify the procedure. Check assignment logs, implementation, protocol deviations, allocation ratios, and any sign of selective assignment.
  • Describe realized balance. Report standardized differences for pre-treatment characteristics (a balance table or Love plot), focusing on the size of differences and their likely prognostic importance rather than pass/fail cutoffs. Variables that respond to the manipulation (perceived quality, emotional reactions) are not baseline covariates.
  • Handle post-randomization selection. Conditioning on treatment receipt, excluding participants, or analyzing complete cases can compromise a randomized comparison when selection is related to treatment and potential outcomes. Keep an intention-to-treat analysis and investigate missingness and noncompliance with an appropriate causal framework.
  • Use covariate adjustment appropriately. Prespecified adjustment for strongly prognostic baseline variables can improve precision, particularly when the realized groups differ on them. It does not remove unmeasured chance imbalance or repair a flawed assignment mechanism. Do not adjust for mediators or other post-treatment variables when estimating a total effect.

The diagnostics flag environmental values and income, which is how the flaw was built in. In a correctly randomized experiment, baseline significance tests have a null hypothesis that is true by design, so about 5% will flag by chance. They are not a certification exercise: read the standardized differences against how strongly each characteristic predicts Y.

When you can’t check what you can’t see

A balance table only describes characteristics you measured. Pilot data and open-ended responses (see Part 4) can help you list candidate pre-treatment characteristics to measure, block on, or adjust for. Keep their causal roles apart from mediators, which are potentially affected by treatment: do not balance-check or adjust for mediators when estimating a total effect. A mediator list becomes relevant for studying pathways, and even then measuring mediators does not establish their causal effects (Module 3, Part 4).

NoteKey questions about your randomization

Randomization does not guarantee identical groups. It guarantees a valid assignment mechanism under which group differences are not systematically driven by potential outcomes. What remains in any one experiment is chance variation. The job of good experimental design and analysis is to understand and manage that variation, not to pretend it does not exist.
