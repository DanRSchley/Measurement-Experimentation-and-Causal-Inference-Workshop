Part 2: What Does “Random Assignment” Actually Require?

We tend to talk about randomization as if it were a button. Click “Randomize,” split people into treatment and control, and treat the result as settled. This page asks how far that button actually takes you.

We follow one experiment throughout. Does an environmental label increase sustainable purchase intention? Eighty participants are split equally between an eco-label condition and a control condition. The researchers believe they are testing whether the label raises consumers’ environmental motivation. What they actually measure is a purchase-intention question.

Every person has two “what-if” outcomes: their score if they saw the label, and their score if they didn’t. Researchers call these potential outcomes (we use them properly in “The Fundamental Problem of Causal Inference” (Module 3, Part 1)). Random assignment means nobody’s what-ifs decide who gets the label. That is exchangeability: before the draw, the two groups are swappable as far as the what-ifs go. It is what makes the comparison between the groups a valid basis for estimating the label’s effect, on the outcome that was measured, among the people who were studied.

That is a real and valuable guarantee, and it is a limited one. Four questions show where it stops:

  1. Does random assignment guarantee similar groups?
  2. Why can chance differences between groups matter?
  3. Does an effect on the DV mean an effect on the construct?
  4. Does the estimate answer the population-level question?

A fifth section asks what balance checks can and cannot tell you. Each question marks a point where a researcher can move from a valid local comparison to a stronger conclusion than the evidence supports.

Does Random Assignment Guarantee Similar Groups?

Think of dealing a shuffled deck into two hands of 26. The shuffle can be perfectly fair and the hands can still differ. On average each hand gets two aces and 13 red cards, but in any one deal the split can be lopsided.

Both hands have 26 cards and 13 red cards, so they are balanced on those two things. But hand 1 holds 3 aces and hand 2 holds 1. With a fair shuffle, the aces split 2–2 only 39% of the time. Lopsided hands don’t mean the shuffle was broken.

If we’re playing a game where aces matter, the imbalance in aces matters. If we’re playing a game where only red cards matter, it may not. The same is true of characteristics that predict our experimental outcome.

The label experiment is the same situation. The 80 participants differ in environmental values, price sensitivity, income, and much more. A fair assignment makes who gets the label unrelated to all of that in expectation, but one particular split can leave the label group a bit greener or less price-sensitive than the control group. And the more characteristics we look at, the more chances at least one of them looks different.

A correctly implemented randomization algorithm draws the assignment in a single step; it never needs extra attempts. What one draw can’t promise is a split that is similar on every characteristic we care about. (Pressing “Randomize” repeatedly until the groups look good is not the same thing, and it isn’t automatically legitimate. Pre-specified rerandomization is a separate design, mentioned at the end.)

Why Can Those Differences Matter?

They matter when the characteristic predicts the outcome. In the simulation below, environmental values is a strong predictor and age is a weak one. A 10-point gap between the groups in environmental values shifts the observed difference by about 0.08 SD; the same gap in age shifts it by about 0.005 SD.

To see the consequence, hold everything else fixed.

  • Fixed: the same 80 participants, ten baseline characteristics (a few strong, most weak), and both potential outcomes for everyone. The label raises the outcome by exactly 0.40 SD for everyone, so the true effect in this sample is 0.40.
  • Repeated: we randomize those 80 people 4,000 times, 40 per condition, and estimate the effect each time. Every assignment is valid.
▶ The ten baseline characteristics and their hypothetical effects on the outcome (SD) 
chars_df <- tibble(
  Characteristic = c("Environmental values", "Price sensitivity", "Prior green behavior", "Social norms", "Household income",
                     "Health motivation", "Peer influence", "Brand trust", "Prior brand use", "Age (older vs. younger)"),
  Effect = c(0.80, 0.60, 0.50, 0.40, 0.30, 0.30, 0.20, 0.20, 0.10, 0.05))
▶ Re-randomize the same 80 participants 4,000 times (≈10 s) 
set.seed(2025)
N_ra <- 80; tau_ra <- 0.40
X_ra <- matrix(rbinom(N_ra * 10, 1, 0.5), nrow = N_ra, dimnames = list(NULL, chars_df$Characteristic))
noise_sd <- sqrt(1 - 0.25 * sum(chars_df$Effect^2))                        # so the population SD of the untreated outcome is 1
Y0_ra <- as.vector(X_ra %*% chars_df$Effect) + rnorm(N_ra, 0, noise_sd)    # untreated potential outcomes (fixed)
Y1_ra <- Y0_ra + tau_ra                                                    # treated potential outcomes (fixed)
sate_ra <- mean(Y1_ra - Y0_ra)                                             # sample average treatment effect

R_ra <- 4000
ra <- as_tibble(t(replicate(R_ra, {
  Tr <- sample(rep(0:1, N_ra / 2))                                         # a valid complete randomization
  Y  <- ifelse(Tr == 1, Y1_ra, Y0_ra)                                      # what we would observe
  c(unadjusted = mean(Y[Tr == 1]) - mean(Y[Tr == 0]),
    adjusted   = unname(coef(lm(Y ~ Tr + X_ra))["Tr"]),                    # pre-planned adjustment for the ten characteristics
    y0_gap     = mean(Y0_ra[Tr == 1]) - mean(Y0_ra[Tr == 0]))              # difference in untreated potential outcomes
})))

est_long <- ra |> pivot_longer(c(unadjusted, adjusted), names_to = "estimator", values_to = "estimate") |>

On average the estimate lands right on the true 0.40. But any single assignment can miss. The typical miss is 0.23 SD, and 20% of assignments miss the true effect by more than 0.30. The participants never changed. Only which of them landed in the label group did.

A valid experiment does not guarantee a precise estimate from one particular assignment. Chance differences in characteristics that predict outcomes are one source of that uncertainty.

Randomization lets us estimate the sample’s average treatment effect without measuring every other determinant of the outcome. But the precision of our particular estimate still depends on how much potential outcomes vary across participants. Important baseline predictors can help us understand and reduce that uncertainty when used appropriately in the design (for example, blocking) or in a pre-planned analysis. Adjusting for the ten characteristics tightened the spread here from 0.23 to 0.20 (the blue curve). Adjustment improves precision. It doesn’t make the original assignment more valid, and it isn’t something to bolt on after spotting an imbalance.

People sometimes say a complex outcome is harder to randomize. Be careful: more latent constructs, more outcome-relevant characteristics, and more interactions between characteristics are three different things. What affects precision is how much of the outcome’s variation the characteristics explain, not how many there are. (The technical notes check this: adding characteristics that don’t matter changes nothing.)

Balance on each characteristic can also hide imbalance on combinations. Suppose purchase intention is high only for participants who are environmentally concerned and not price sensitive. Both groups below are 50% concerned and 50% price sensitive, so a balance table that checks one characteristic at a time looks perfect.

But 15 of the 40 label participants are in the high-intention combination, against 5 of the 40 controls. If that combination scores 2 points higher on the 7-point scale, the groups differ by about 0.5 points before any treatment effect. This is a constructed, extreme case, and valid randomization can produce it. It shows what one-at-a-time balance tables can’t see; it isn’t evidence that the randomization failed.

In general, imbalance matters more with small samples, strong predictors, interactions, unequal allocation or clustering, and post-randomization selection (see Module 2, Part 3).

Does an Effect on the DV Mean an Effect on the Construct?

Back to the label experiment. The researchers want to know whether the label raises environmental motivation. But the DV is a purchase-intention item (How likely are you to buy this product?). A participant’s answer reflects at least two things: environmental motivation, and practical willingness to buy given price, income, convenience, and quality. Different characteristics drive each. Environmental values mostly feed motivation; price sensitivity and income mostly feed practical willingness.

The DV rises by 0.3 points. The same 0.3 would appear if the label had raised both components by 0.3. Nothing is wrong with the randomization: the label really did change purchase intention. What the data can’t say is which component moved, and perfectly balanced groups would leave that ambiguity untouched.

Randomization can establish that treatment changed the measured outcome without establishing which latent construct changed.

This is a different problem from chance imbalance. It is a measurement question: does the DV represent the construct we intend to study (Module 1)? The two concerns are related only loosely. Components with different antecedents mean several characteristics can contribute to chance imbalance, but a more complex DV doesn’t make assignment less valid.

Does the Estimate Answer the Population-Level Question?

Even a precise, correctly interpreted estimate describes the people who were studied. Three quantities need to stay apart: the observed estimate from our experiment, the sample average treatment effect (the average effect among these 80 participants, given their what-ifs), and the population average treatment effect (the average effect among the consumers we care about).

The first gap is assignment uncertainty: the sample effect is fixed by who is in the study, and the random draw decides how far our estimate lands from it. That is the spread you saw above. The second gap is sampling and generalization uncertainty: the people in the study may not represent the people we care about.

Suppose the label works strongly for environmentally motivated consumers (+0.8 points) and weakly for everyone else (+0.2). If 60% of our sample is environmentally motivated but only 30% of the target population is, the sample effect is 0.56 while the population effect is 0.38. Perfectly balanced label and control groups would not change that. (Measurement can also differ across populations, so the same DV may not mean the same thing everywhere.)

Randomizing treatment does not randomize who enters the study, and it does not guarantee that an effect transports to another population.

Why Detecting an Effect Doesn’t Establish Comparability

The last question is about evidence for the assignment process itself. Two scenarios show the limits of what the data can tell us.

▶ Scenario A: a valid randomization with a real effect (3,000 experiments) 
set.seed(9)
tau_a <- 0.6
sc_a <- as_tibble(t(replicate(3000, {
  Tr <- sample(rep(0:1, 40)); X <- matrix(rbinom(80 * 10, 1, 0.5), nrow = 80)
  Y  <- tau_a * Tr + as.vector(X %*% chars_df$Effect) + rnorm(80, 0, noise_sd)
  c(reject = t.test(Y[Tr == 1], Y[Tr == 0])$p.value < 0.05,
    strict_balance_fails = max(abs(apply(X, 2, cor, Tr))) >= 0.10 - 1e-9,
    estimate = mean(Y[Tr == 1]) - mean(Y[Tr == 0]))
})))
kable(tibble(`Experiments detecting the effect (p < .05)` = percent(mean(sc_a$reject), accuracy = 1),
             `Experiments missing the strict 0.10 balance criterion` = percent(mean(sc_a$strict_balance_fails), accuracy = 0.1),
             `Average estimate (true effect 0.6)` = sprintf("%.2f", mean(sc_a$estimate))),
      caption = "Scenario A: valid randomization, 80 participants")

A valid randomization. In a simulation with 80 participants, a true effect of 0.6 SD, and correct assignment, the effect is detected in 75% of experiments, even though 99.7% of them miss a strict balance rule (some characteristic with a treatment correlation of 0.10 or more). The estimates average 0.60. A correctly randomized experiment doesn’t need to pass a baseline test.

A defective assignment. Now suppose assignment quietly depends on a characteristic that also drives the outcome. For example, the label version was advertised on an environmental forum, so people with strong environmental values were somewhat more likely to get it. The true label effect is zero. The researchers measure environmental values with error (a short questionnaire) and run the usual diagnostic: does the measured proxy predict condition? Any “significant” treatment effect is now a false positive.

▶ Scenario B: defective assignment, zero true effect, N from 40 to 1,280 (≈40 s) 
sim_flaw <- function(N, gamma = 0.3, beta = 0.8, rels = c(good = 0.6, poor = 0.3), nrep = 2000) {
  out <- t(replicate(nrep, {
    U  <- rnorm(N); Tr <- rbinom(N, 1, plogis(gamma * U))                  # defective: assignment depends on U
    if (length(unique(Tr)) < 2) return(rep(NA_real_, 4))
    Y  <- beta * U + rnorm(N)                                              # true treatment effect is ZERO
    det <- sapply(rels, function(rel) {
      W <- U + rnorm(N, 0, sqrt((1 - rel) / rel))                          # proxy with reliability `rel`
      summary(glm(Tr ~ W, family = binomial))$coefficients["W", 4] < 0.05 })
    c(false_pos = t.test(Y[Tr == 1], Y[Tr == 0])$p.value < 0.05, det, bias = mean(Y[Tr == 1]) - mean(Y[Tr == 0]))
  }))
  tibble(N = N, `False positive (treatment test)` = mean(out[, 1], na.rm = TRUE),
         `Diagnostic detects it: decent proxy` = mean(out[, 2], na.rm = TRUE),
         `Diagnostic detects it: poor proxy` = mean(out[, 3], na.rm = TRUE), bias = mean(out[, 4], na.rm = TRUE))
}
set.seed(3)
flaw_res <- bind_rows(lapply(c(40, 80, 160, 320, 640, 1280), sim_flaw))
flaw_long <- flaw_res |> pivot_longer(-c(N, bias), names_to = "what", values_to = "rate") |>

The orange line is how often a convincing-looking effect appears when there is none. The blue lines are how often the balance diagnostic notices the problem. Noisier measurement of the baseline characteristic weakens the diagnostic, and a characteristic nobody measured can’t be checked at all.

A significant treatment-outcome relationship doesn’t validate the assignment mechanism, and a non-significant balance test doesn’t show that important differences are absent. Equally, a correctly randomized experiment doesn’t need to pass a baseline significance test for its causal inference to be valid. Whether assignment worked is established by how it was carried out, not inferred from the results.

What Can You Actually Conclude After Randomizing?

Each claim a researcher might make needs evidence beyond the one before it.

1. “The label changed the observed DV in this experiment.” A correctly implemented randomized design supports this, with an appropriate uncertainty analysis. Chance imbalance doesn’t undermine it; it is part of the uncertainty you report.

2. “The label changed the construct we intended to study.” This needs evidence that the DV represents that construct and that other constructs feeding the measure don’t explain the result.

3. “The label will have the same effect in the population we care about.” This needs a justification for generalizing beyond the participants, including how the sample was drawn and whether the effect differs across people.

4. “The statistical result proves our randomization worked.” It doesn’t. Assignment is established from the research process (how it was implemented and documented), not from significant treatment tests or non-significant balance tests.

None of this is a reason to distrust randomized experiments. It is a reason to be precise about what they establish.

Optional Technical Notes

These notes hold the calculations behind the claims above. Nothing in the main argument depends on reading them.

NoteUnderstanding randomization: how many riffle shuffles are enough?

For a real shuffle, the question “is it random yet?” has an exact answer (Bayer & Diaconis, 1992). The figure shows how far the order of a deck is from perfectly random after k riffle shuffles. A score of 0 means every ordering is equally likely; a score near 1 means the shuffle leaves the deck stuck in a small corner of all possible orders.

▶ Exact total variation distance for GSR riffle shuffles (code) 
# Eulerian counts E(n, r) for r = 1..n: the number of permutations of n cards with exactly
# r rising sequences. Recurrence: E(1,1) = 1; E(n,r) = r * E(n-1,r) + (n-r+1) * E(n-1,r-1),
# with values outside 1..n-1 equal to zero. The counts for each n sum to n!.
eulerian_counts <- function(n) {
  E <- 1
  if (n > 1) for (m in 2:n) {
    r     <- seq_len(m)
    prev  <- c(E, rep(0, m - length(E)))                    # E(m-1, r)
    prev1 <- c(0, E, rep(0, m - 1 - length(E)))             # E(m-1, r-1)
    E <- r * prev + (m - r + 1) * prev1
  }
  E
}

# Exact total variation distance from the uniform distribution on the n! orderings after k GSR
# riffle shuffles (Bayer & Diaconis, 1992). Probabilities are computed on the log scale.
riffle_tv <- function(n, k) {
  a    <- 2^k
  r    <- seq_len(n)
  P_r  <- exp(lchoose(a + n - r, n) - n * log(a))           # probability of ONE permutation with r rising sequences
  0.5 * sum(eulerian_counts(n) * abs(P_r - exp(-lgamma(n + 1))))
}

One riffle shuffle leaves even a 5-card deck far from random (distance 0.775). For 52 cards the distance is about 0.334 after seven shuffles, 0.167 after eight, and 0.085 after nine. The familiar “seven shuffles” rule marks the point where things start to settle down fast, not the point of perfect randomness.

For the mathematically inclined: with \(a = 2^k\), a permutation of n cards with r rising sequences has probability \(\binom{a+n-r}{n}/a^{n}\) after k riffle shuffles, and the distance is \(\tfrac12\sum_{r=1}^{n} E(n,r)\,\bigl|P(n,k,r) - 1/n!\bigr|\), where \(E(n,r)\) counts permutations with r rising sequences. This is a property of the whole distribution of decks a shuffle can produce; you can’t read it off one deck. It is an analogy for a physical shuffle. A software randomization draws the assignment directly from the intended distribution.

NoteHow likely is strict balance when we check many characteristics?
▶ Correlation between treatment and a binary characteristic, from group counts 
# Correlation between treatment (exactly m treated, m control) and a binary characteristic, given
# that `a` treated and `b` control participants have X = 1. Constant characteristics (p = 0 or 1)
# have no group difference, so r = 0 rather than NaN.
treat_cov_cor <- function(a, b, m) {
  p1 <- a / m; p0 <- b / m; p <- (a + b) / (2 * m)
  ifelse(p == 0 | p == 1, 0, (p1 - p0) / (2 * sqrt(p * (1 - p))))
}
treat_cov_cor_ex <- treat_cov_cor(22, 18, 40)      # 55% vs. 45% high among 40 + 40 participants

Take 80 participants, 40 per condition, with half the sample high on some characteristic. Perfect balance puts 20 high people in each group. A perfectly valid assignment might put 22 in one and 18 in the other, 55% versus 45%. With equal groups the correlation between treatment and the characteristic equals that gap, here r = 0.10.

Now suppose we want every one of D independent characteristics to have \(|r| < 0.10\), under valid complete randomization. The probability is the one-characteristic probability raised to the power D.

▶ Exact probability of meeting the marginal-balance criterion (code) 
# q(N): probability that ONE independent Bernoulli(0.5) characteristic has |cor(T, X)| < thresh
# under complete randomization with N/2 per arm. The inequality is strict; exact ties
# (|r| = thresh) count as failing, with a tiny tolerance to guard against floating-point error.
q_balance <- function(N, thresh = 0.10) {
  stopifnot(N %% 2 == 0)
  m  <- N / 2
  pm <- dbinom(0:m, m, 0.5)                          # P(a) and P(b): Binomial(N/2, 0.5)
  g  <- expand.grid(a = 0:m, b = 0:m)
  r  <- treat_cov_cor(g$a, g$b, m)
  sum(pm[g$a + 1] * pm[g$b + 1] * (abs(r) < thresh - 1e-9))
}

# P(pass on all D independent characteristics) = q(N)^D
p_balance_all <- function(N, D, thresh = 0.10) q_balance(N, thresh)^D

These are not the probability that randomization “worked,” that the estimate is unbiased, or that the estimate is close to the truth: the assignment is valid throughout. They illustrate only that checking more characteristics gives more chances to see a difference. And \(2^D\) counts the possible joint profiles of D binary characteristics (1,024 for ten); it is not a sample size anyone needs.

NoteDoes having more characteristics widen the spread of estimates?

Not by itself. In a completely randomized experiment with equal groups, the spread of the unadjusted estimate is proportional to the SD of the untreated outcome, whatever produced that variability. Writing the outcome as \(Y = \tau T + \sum_j \beta_j C_j + \varepsilon\), the unadjusted estimate equals the treatment effect, plus imbalance in each characteristic weighted by its effect, plus leftover noise. The table uses the same 80 participants and the same 2,000 assignments in every row.

NoteWhen the characteristics are correlated with each other

In real data characteristics travel together (people who care about the environment also tend to care about health and social norms). Correlation doesn’t automatically help or hurt precision, and correlated predictors aren’t each an independent source of bias. The simulation uses ten characteristics, complete randomization, and an explicit untreated outcome (characteristics times effects of 0.3, plus noise with SD 1), so the swing from chance imbalance is expressed in SD of that outcome.

  Sigma <- matrix(rho, D, D); diag(Sigma) <- 1

}

NoteSpecification of the defective-assignment simulation
  • Assignment: each participant is treated with probability \(\text{logistic}(0.3\,U)\), where \(U\) is a standard-normal baseline characteristic (a modest defect).
  • Outcome: \(Y = 0.8\,U + \text{noise}\) (SD 1), with no treatment effect, so every significant treatment test is a false positive.
  • Measured: a proxy \(W = U + \text{noise}\) with reliability 0.6 (“decent”) or 0.3 (“poor”).
  • Statistics: the unadjusted treatment test (false-positive rate), and a logistic regression of treatment on \(W\) (diagnostic detection rate, p < .05). 2,000 experiments per sample size, fixed seed.
