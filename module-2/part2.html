Part 2: What Does “Random Assignment” Actually Require?

What Does Random Assignment Actually Guarantee?

We tend to talk about randomization as if it were a button. Click “Randomize,” split people into treatment and control, and treat the result as settled. This page asks how far that button actually takes you.

We follow one experiment throughout. Does an environmental label increase sustainable purchase intention? Eighty participants are split equally between an eco-label condition and a control condition. The researchers believe they are testing whether the label raises consumers’ environmental motivation. What they actually measure is a purchase-intention question.

Every person has two “what-if” outcomes: their score if they saw the label, and their score if they didn’t. Researchers call these potential outcomes (we use them properly in “The Fundamental Problem of Causal Inference” (Module 3, Part 1)). Random assignment means nobody’s what-ifs decide who gets the label. That is exchangeability: before the draw, the two groups are swappable as far as the what-ifs go. It is what makes the comparison a valid basis for estimating the label’s effect, on the outcome that was measured, among the people who were studied.

Randomization is a procedure. Exchangeability is what a valid procedure gives you. Balance is what you actually see in the one experiment you ran.

That is a real guarantee, and a limited one. To see how limited, we start with something simpler than people: a deck of cards. Then we move to consumers, ask what a chance imbalance does to an estimate, and finish with what randomization cannot establish however well it is done.

Shuffling Cards: Why Size and Procedure Matter

Take a deck in its original order and shuffle it once. Is the resulting order random? What if you shuffle twice? And does the answer change when the deck has 5 cards instead of 52?

“Random” here means every possible order is equally likely. A deck of n cards can be arranged in n! different orders:

A real shuffle can’t reach all of those orders equally in one go. Under the Gilbert–Shannon–Reeds model of riffle shuffling, how many shuffles it takes to get close to a uniformly random order depends on how big the deck is (Bayer & Diaconis, 1992). We measure “close” with the total variation distance from uniform: zero means every ordering of the deck is equally likely, and larger values mean the shuffling process still favors some arrangements over others.

▶ Exact total variation distance for GSR riffle shuffles (code) 
# Eulerian counts E(n, r) for r = 1..n: the number of permutations of n cards with exactly
# r rising sequences. Recurrence: E(1,1) = 1; E(n,r) = r * E(n-1,r) + (n-r+1) * E(n-1,r-1),
# with values outside 1..n-1 equal to zero. The counts for each n sum to n!.
eulerian_counts <- function(n) {
  E <- 1
  if (n > 1) for (m in 2:n) {
    r     <- seq_len(m)
    prev  <- c(E, rep(0, m - length(E)))                    # E(m-1, r)
    prev1 <- c(0, E, rep(0, m - 1 - length(E)))             # E(m-1, r-1)
    E <- r * prev + (m - r + 1) * prev1
  }
  E
}

# Exact total variation distance from the uniform distribution on the n! orderings after k GSR
# riffle shuffles (Bayer & Diaconis, 1992). Probabilities are computed on the log scale.
riffle_tv <- function(n, k) {
  a    <- 2^k
  r    <- seq_len(n)
  P_r  <- exp(lchoose(a + n - r, n) - n * log(a))           # probability of ONE permutation with r rising sequences
  0.5 * sum(eulerian_counts(n) * abs(P_r - exp(-lgamma(n + 1))))
}

NoteTechnical note: the exact calculation and a table

The distance is computed exactly. With \(a = 2^k\), a permutation of n cards with r rising sequences has probability \(\binom{a+n-r}{n}/a^{n}\) after k riffle shuffles, and the distance is \(\tfrac12\sum_{r=1}^{n} E(n,r)\,\bigl|P(n,k,r) - 1/n!\bigr|\), where \(E(n,r)\) is the Eulerian number counting permutations with r rising sequences. It is a property of the whole distribution of decks a shuffle can produce; you can’t read it off one deck.

Even a Randomized Deck Can Produce Unequal Hands

Now suppose the deck was shuffled well, and we deal it into two hands of 26. Two different questions are now in play:

  1. Was the deck randomized adequately? This concerns the mechanism: the distribution of arrangements it can produce.
  2. How similar are the hands we got? This concerns the realized allocation: the one arrangement that actually came up.

On average each hand gets two aces and 13 red cards. But in any one deal the split can be lopsided.

Both hands have 26 cards and 13 red cards, so they are balanced on those two things. But hand 1 holds 3 aces and hand 2 holds 1. With a fair shuffle the aces split 2–2 only 39% of the time, and all four land in one hand 11% of the time.

We can have a poorly randomized deck that produces apparently balanced hands, or a perfectly randomized deck that produces unbalanced hands. Examining the hands alone cannot establish whether the shuffle was fair.

Whether an imbalance matters depends on the game. If we’re playing a game where aces matter, the imbalance in aces matters. If we’re playing a game where only red cards matter, it may not. The same is true of characteristics that predict our experimental outcome.

Where the analogy stops. A correctly implemented computer randomization draws the assignment from its intended distribution in one step. Adding more baseline characteristics doesn’t make that mechanism less random, and no one needs to “press Randomize” more times. (Pressing it repeatedly until the groups look good is not the same thing, and it isn’t automatically legitimate; pre-specified rerandomization is a separate design.) What carries over is this: the number of possible participant profiles can be enormous, one realized allocation can differ across many characteristics, and those differences can matter when the characteristics predict potential outcomes. Randomization doesn’t guarantee the kind of balance researchers often assume.

From Cards to Consumers: How Complex Is the Outcome-Generating Process?

A playing card has properties we can inspect: its color, suit, rank, whether it is an ace. Whether two hands are meaningfully similar depends on which of those properties matter for the game.

Participants are more complicated. They differ in environmental values, price sensitivity, habits, social influences, and more. Which differences matter depends on the outcome we are studying. Some outcomes may be strongly affected by relatively few outcome-relevant baseline characteristics; others depend on a much richer combination. As that number grows, so does the number of possible participant profiles and the number of ways two randomized groups can differ.

Here are two hypothetical outcome-generating processes. They aren’t claims about how real psychological constructs work, and a more complex construct doesn’t necessarily have more determinants. They show what changes when the number of relevant characteristics grows. In both, not all characteristics matter equally; the effect columns give each one’s hypothetical effect on the outcome, in standard deviations.

With only 80 participants, how can the label and control groups possibly represent all 1,024 profiles? They can’t, and that doesn’t invalidate random assignment. But the expanding profile space makes it easier to see why a finite allocation can contain meaningful differences on outcome-relevant characteristics.

Why Exact Coverage Gives Way to Approximate Orthogonality

Since we can’t cover every profile, what kind of similarity between the groups should we hope for? Start with one characteristic.

▶ Correlation between treatment and a binary characteristic, from group counts 
# Correlation between treatment (exactly m treated, m control) and a binary characteristic, given
# that `a` treated and `b` control participants have X = 1. Constant characteristics (p = 0 or 1)
# have no group difference, so r = 0 rather than NaN.
treat_cov_cor <- function(a, b, m) {
  p1 <- a / m; p0 <- b / m; p <- (a + b) / (2 * m)
  ifelse(p == 0 | p == 1, 0, (p1 - p0) / (2 * sqrt(p * (1 - p))))
}
treat_cov_cor_ex <- treat_cov_cor(22, 18, 40)      # 55% vs. 45% high among 40 + 40 participants

Take 80 participants, 40 per condition, and say exactly 40 have high environmental concern. Perfect balance puts 20 high-concern people in each group, 50% versus 50%. A perfectly valid random assignment might put 22 in one group and 18 in the other: 55% versus 45%, a ten-percentage-point gap from moving two people. In this balanced binary setup the correlation between treatment and the characteristic equals that gap, so here r = 0.10.

Now suppose we care about ten characteristics rather than one. Even if each is reasonably likely to be balanced, the chance that all ten are balanced can be much lower. We can compute this exactly in a deliberately simple setup: equal-sized groups, D independent binary characteristics each “high” for half the sample, valid randomization every time, and “balanced” meaning \(|r| < 0.10\).

▶ Exact probability of meeting the marginal-balance criterion (code) 
# q(N): probability that ONE independent Bernoulli(0.5) characteristic has |cor(T, X)| < thresh
# under complete randomization with N/2 per arm. The inequality is strict; exact ties
# (|r| = thresh) count as failing, with a tiny tolerance to guard against floating-point error.
q_balance <- function(N, thresh = 0.10) {
  stopifnot(N %% 2 == 0)
  m  <- N / 2
  pm <- dbinom(0:m, m, 0.5)                          # P(a) and P(b): Binomial(N/2, 0.5)
  g  <- expand.grid(a = 0:m, b = 0:m)
  r  <- treat_cov_cor(g$a, g$b, m)
  sum(pm[g$a + 1] * pm[g$b + 1] * (abs(r) < thresh - 1e-9))
}

# P(pass on all D independent characteristics) = q(N)^D
p_balance_all <- function(N, D, thresh = 0.10) q_balance(N, thresh)^D

bal_tab <- expand.grid(N = c(80, 200, 400), D = c(1, 2, 5, 10)) |>
  mutate(p = mapply(p_balance_all, N, D))

At 80 participants, the chance of meeting the criterion on every characteristic is 32% for two characteristics and 0.34% for ten.

Every assignment here is genuinely randomized. These probabilities refer only to achieving a strict balance criterion; they are not the probability that exchangeability holds, that the randomization was valid, or that the estimate is close to the truth. They show two things: more characteristics make strict simultaneous balance harder, and a larger sample makes it easier.

The statistical term for what this figure is about is approximate orthogonality: small observed relationships between treatment assignment and baseline characteristics. Under valid randomization the expected relationship is zero, but in any finite sample it generally isn’t exactly zero. For one characteristic, its typical size shrinks at roughly the \(1/\sqrt{N}\) rate.

As N increases, realized balance becomes more likely. Exchangeability does not become increasingly valid; it was supplied by the assignment mechanism at any N.

When Does Realized Imbalance Actually Matter?

The previous section explains why exact balance on many characteristics is hard. Why does it matter for the estimate? An earlier lesson already gave us the tool: omitted-variable bias (Module 1, Part 2).

Just as different properties of playing cards matter for different games, different participant characteristics matter for different dependent variables. When those characteristics happen to correlate with treatment in the realized assignment, they can contribute to the estimated effect in proportion to how strongly they predict the outcome.

The omitted-variable equation

In the label experiment, let \(T\) indicate the label (1) versus control (0), \(C\) indicate environmental concern, and \(Y\) indicate purchase intention. Suppose

\[Y = \tau\,T + \beta\,C + \varepsilon .\]

A researcher who regresses \(Y\) on \(T\) alone gets, in a realized dataset,

\[\hat\tau = \underbrace{\tau}_{\text{true effect}} + \underbrace{\beta\,\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(T,C)}{\operatorname{Var}(T)}}_{\text{omitted-variable contribution}} + \underbrace{\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(T,\varepsilon)}{\operatorname{Var}(T)}}_{\text{noise}} .\]

The middle term is the familiar omitted-variable contribution. For a binary treatment, \(\operatorname{Cov}(T,C)/\operatorname{Var}(T)\) is simply the difference in mean \(C\) between the treatment and control groups.

Here is a concrete case. Of 80 participants, 40 per condition, 22 label participants and 18 controls have high environmental concern, a difference of 0.10 in the share of high-concern participants. High concern raises purchase intention by 0.80 points, and the label’s true effect is 0.40 points. The imbalance contributes \(0.80 \times 0.10 = 0.08\) points, so with no other noise the unadjusted effect is \(0.40 + 0.08 = 0.48\).

The same 10-point gap on a weakly prognostic characteristic, such as age (effect 0.05), would contribute only 0.005 points. What matters is the size of the gap and how strongly the characteristic predicts the outcome.

Random imbalance versus systematic confounding

The algebra is the same in two very different situations. What differs is the assignment mechanism.

  • Valid randomization. \(\operatorname{Cov}(T,C)\) may be nonzero in one realized allocation, as in the 22-versus-18 example, but its expected value over possible allocations is zero. The omitted-variable contribution is part of randomization uncertainty. It isn’t systematic omitted-variable bias, and it doesn’t invalidate the experiment.
  • Flawed assignment. If allocation systematically depends on \(C\) (say the label version was advertised on an environmental forum), \(\operatorname{Cov}(T,C)\) need not average to zero. Omitting \(C\) now produces systematic confounding bias under this outcome model.

In any one assignment, omitting the prognostic characteristics moves the unadjusted estimate up or down: the typical miss is 0.23 SD, and 20% of assignments miss the true effect by more than 0.30. A valid experiment does not guarantee a precise estimate from one particular assignment. Chance differences in characteristics that predict outcomes are one source of that uncertainty.

Randomization lets us estimate the sample’s average treatment effect without measuring every other determinant of the outcome. A pre-specified adjustment for important baseline predictors, or blocking on them, can improve precision: adjusting for the ten characteristics tightened the spread from 0.23 to 0.20 (the blue curve). Two further points keep the number of characteristics in perspective:

  • Stronger prognostic characteristics make a given imbalance more consequential, and interactions can make balance on individual characteristics insufficient (below).
  • If total variability in potential outcomes is held constant, adding more determinants doesn’t mechanically increase the variance of an unadjusted randomized estimate (see the technical note).

What Randomization Still Cannot Establish

Three further limits remain even when assignment is valid and the omitted-variable contribution is small. The first is easy to confuse with what we just covered.

A participant’s answer reflects at least two things: environmental motivation, and practical willingness to buy given price, income, convenience, and quality.

Even a precise, correctly interpreted estimate describes the people who were studied. Three quantities need to stay apart: the observed estimate from our experiment, the sample average treatment effect (the average effect among these 80 participants, given their what-ifs), and the population average treatment effect (the average effect among the consumers we care about).

The first gap is assignment uncertainty: the sample effect is fixed by who is in the study, and the random draw decides how far our estimate lands from it. That is the spread you saw above. Randomizing treatment does not randomize who enters the study, and it does not guarantee that an effect transports to another population.

What Researchers Should Do

What can you actually conclude after randomizing?

Each claim a researcher might make needs evidence beyond the one before it.

1. “The label changed the observed DV in this experiment.” A correctly implemented randomized design supports this, with an appropriate uncertainty analysis. Chance imbalance doesn’t undermine it; it is part of the uncertainty you report.

2. “The label changed the construct we intended to study.” This needs evidence that the DV represents that construct and that other constructs feeding the measure don’t explain the result.

3. “The label will have the same effect in the population we care about.” This needs a justification for generalizing beyond the participants, including how the sample was drawn and whether the effect differs across people.

4. “The statistical result proves our randomization worked.” It doesn’t. Assignment is established from the research process (how it was implemented and documented), not from significant treatment tests or non-significant balance tests.

None of this is a reason to distrust randomized experiments. It is a reason to be precise about what they establish.

NoteSpecification of the defective-assignment simulation
  • Assignment: each participant is treated with probability \(\text{logistic}(0.3\,U)\), where \(U\) is a standard-normal baseline characteristic (a modest defect).
  • Outcome: \(Y = 0.8\,U + \text{noise}\) (SD 1), with no treatment effect, so every significant treatment test is a false positive.
  • Measured: a proxy \(W = U + \text{noise}\) with reliability 0.6 (“decent”) or 0.3 (“poor”).
  • Statistics: the unadjusted treatment test (false-positive rate), and a logistic regression of treatment on \(W\) (diagnostic detection rate, p < .05). 2,000 experiments per sample size, fixed seed.
