Part 1: Discriminant Validity

The Classical Test Theory Starting Point

Every measurement model in the social sciences begins from the same core assumption: the observed score you record is a sum of the true score and measurement error.

\[X_\text{observed} = T_\text{true} + \varepsilon\]

Classical test theory (CTT) assumes the error term \(\varepsilon\) is random and symmetric: equally likely to push the observed score up or down, averaging to zero. Under this assumption, observed scores are noisy but unbiased indicators of the latent construct you care about.

But this assumption is almost always violated in practice. The measurement error is not symmetric; it is contaminated by something else. The six parts in this tutorial are six different ways that “something else” creeps into your observed score:

  • When your scale picks up a second construct (Part 1), ε includes that construct’s variance and is no longer random.
  • When an omitted variable drives both your predictor and outcome (Part 2), the “error” in your regression coefficient is systematic, not random.
  • When scale items shift their baseline across groups (Part 3), ε differs systematically between conditions.
  • When your sample mixes two populations (Part 4), ε has a bimodal structure that standard models cannot represent.
  • When your data contain influential outliers (Part 5), ε includes extreme values that disproportionately distort regression coefficients.
  • When your outcome scale has hard bounds (Part 6), ε is truncated at the ends of the scale, so observed means are pulled toward the middle.

Discriminant validity failure is the case where the error in your observed scale is not random noise but structured variance from a second latent construct. Suppose we are trying to measure Green Purchase Intentions (GPI), but our measure also captures a related construct, Environmental Concern (EC). Then the CTT equation becomes

\[X_\text{GPI} = T_\text{true GPI} + \lambda \cdot T_\text{EC} + \varepsilon_\text{random},\]

where \(\lambda\) is how much Environmental Concern bleeds into your GPI items. This is no longer a pure measure of one construct.

NoteThe same problem in other disciplines

The same issue, an observable index picking up several latent sources of variance, appears under different names across fields:

  • Econometrics / identification: a coefficient is unidentified when the predictor of interest cannot be separated from another variable that moves with it.
  • Epidemiology: a biomarker elevated by both inflammation and metabolic syndrome lacks specificity.
  • Psychometrics: construct contamination (the scale measures variance beyond the intended construct); its mirror image is construct deficiency.
  • Political science: composite indices (democracy, corruption) often collapse several dimensions into one number.

Whenever a single observed variable (or a scale) proxies a latent construct, you need to verify it is specific to that construct.

The Study Scenario

We simulate a green marketing experiment with 400 participants, asking whether eco-friendly brand messaging increases green purchase intentions. Half saw standard product advertising (Control); half saw ads emphasising eco-friendly credentials (Green Marketing). All constructs are measured on 7-point Likert scales:

Construct Abbreviation # Items Role
Environmental Concern EC 4 Covariate
Green Purchase Intention GPI 4 Dependent Variable
Brand Attitude BA 3 Covariate

The hidden problem we planted: the GPI items are nearly indistinguishable from the EC items (the latent correlation is 0.93). Any effect of the campaign on GPI is then hard to interpret: are participants more willing to buy, or just more environmentally concerned? In all six parts, the core problem is the same: the observed Y is an imperfect, contaminated proxy for the latent Y you want to study.

ImportantThese statistical tests only apply in specific situations

Discriminant validity is a concern whenever two related constructs are in your study, but the tests below (HTMT and DVI) require multi-item Likert scales with at least four items per construct. They do not apply to single-item outcomes, willingness to pay, choice data, or behavior. If your DV is one of those, discriminant validity is still your responsibility; it is just harder to detect, and the last section of this part covers what to do.

Simulating the Data

NoteSimulation details and descriptives

We use MASS::mvrnorm() to generate correlated latent factor scores, create item scores from those factors plus random error, and round to a 7-point scale. EC and GPI are correlated at 0.93 in the population, well above the 0.90 threshold used in HTMT-based tests (Henseler et al., 2015).

▶ Simulate green marketing dataset (n=400) 
set.seed(2025)
n <- 400

# ── Step 1: Assign participants to conditions ──────────────────────────────────
treatment <- sample(c(0L, 1L), size = n, replace = TRUE)

# ── Step 2: Define the true factor correlation matrix ─────────────────────────
# EC and GPI are correlated at .93 — too high for discriminant validity
# EC–BA and GPI–BA are at more typical, moderate levels
phi_pop <- matrix(
  c(1.00, 0.93, 0.45,   # EC row
    0.93, 1.00, 0.50,   # GPI row
    0.45, 0.50, 1.00),  # BA row
  nrow = 3, byrow = TRUE,
  dimnames = list(c("EC", "GPI", "BA"), c("EC", "GPI", "BA"))
)

# ── Step 3: Generate latent factor scores ─────────────────────────────────────
latent_base <- MASS::mvrnorm(n = n, mu = c(0, 0, 0), Sigma = phi_pop)

EC_lat  <- latent_base[, 1]
GPI_lat <- latent_base[, 2] + 0.40 * treatment  # Green marketing raises GPI by .40 SD
BA_lat  <- latent_base[, 3]

# ── Step 4: Define item loadings ──────────────────────────────────────────────
# These represent how strongly each item reflects its latent construct
lambda_EC  <- c(0.78, 0.82, 0.74, 0.76)
lambda_GPI <- c(0.80, 0.76, 0.82, 0.78)
lambda_BA  <- c(0.72, 0.76, 0.70)

# ── Step 5: Generate continuous item scores (latent score + measurement error) ─
gen_items <- function(latent, loadings) {
  sapply(loadings, function(lam) {
    lam * latent + sqrt(1 - lam^2) * rnorm(length(latent))
  })
}

EC_cont  <- gen_items(EC_lat,  lambda_EC)
GPI_cont <- gen_items(GPI_lat, lambda_GPI)
BA_cont  <- gen_items(BA_lat,  lambda_BA)

# ── Step 6: Round to 7-point Likert scale ─────────────────────────────────────
# Cut the continuous distribution into 7 ordered categories
to_likert7 <- function(x) {
  z <- (x - mean(x)) / sd(x)   # standardise each item
  breaks <- c(-Inf, -1.5, -0.75, -0.25, 0.25, 0.75, 1.5, Inf)
  as.integer(cut(z, breaks = breaks, labels = 1:7))
}

EC_lik  <- apply(EC_cont,  2, to_likert7)
GPI_lik <- apply(GPI_cont, 2, to_likert7)
BA_lik  <- apply(BA_cont,  2, to_likert7)

# ── Step 7: Assemble the final data frame ─────────────────────────────────────
df <- data.frame(
  id        = 1:n,
  condition = factor(treatment, levels = c(0, 1),
                     labels = c("Control", "Green Marketing")),
  EC1  = EC_lik[, 1], EC2  = EC_lik[, 2],
  EC3  = EC_lik[, 3], EC4  = EC_lik[, 4],
  GPI1 = GPI_lik[, 1], GPI2 = GPI_lik[, 2],
  GPI3 = GPI_lik[, 3], GPI4 = GPI_lik[, 4],
  BA1  = BA_lik[, 1], BA2  = BA_lik[, 2], BA3  = BA_lik[, 3]
)

First Look: Convergent and Discriminant Validity in the Raw Data

Before any formal test, look at the raw correlation structure of the 11 items. Convergent validity: items within a scale should correlate strongly. Discriminant validity: items from different scales should correlate noticeably less. The warning sign is a between-scale block that looks as dark as the within-scale blocks.

Factor Analysis and Convergent Validity

Factor analysis starts from the same CTT logic: each item is a noisy reflection of a latent construct plus error. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) lets the data reveal how many factors are needed; confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) tests a specific, theory-driven structure. Both ask the convergent validity question: do the items meant to measure the same construct cluster together?

NoteExploratory factor analysis (scree plot, loadings, variance explained)

EFA makes no assumption about which items belong together. Parallel analysis compares your eigenvalues to those from random data of the same size; retain factors only where your eigenvalue clearly exceeds the random baseline.

WarningWhat the results reveal: a two-part story

Convergent validity is fine. Loadings are strong (> 0.70) for EC, GPI, and BA items; the items cohere within each scale.

Discriminant validity is not. The EC and GPI factors correlate at about 0.93 (the ~~ rows in the CFA summary()). Convergent and discriminant validity are distinct assessments and can give opposite verdicts: items can hang together within each scale while the scales themselves remain statistically indistinguishable.

Discriminant Validity Testing Methods

The items converge within their intended scales. The question now is whether the scales can be distinguished from each other. Two formal methods are available for multi-item Likert scales. The explanation of each is below; running it in R is in the dropdown.

Method 1: HTMT Analysis

HTMT is the Heterotrait-Monotrait ratio of correlations. Monotrait correlations are between items of the same scale; heterotrait correlations are between items of different scales. HTMT asks how large the between-scale correlations are relative to the within-scale ones. Near 1.0, the scales are not distinguishable.

  • HTMT < 0.85: discriminant validity supported (strict)
  • HTMT < 0.90: supported (lenient)
  • HTMT ≥ 0.90: violated

We use HTMT2, the updated index based on the geometric mean of within-scale correlations, which is now recommended over the original.

Method 2: The DVI Method (Pieters et al., 2025)

HTMT is a good screen but has two gaps: its thresholds are rules of thumb, not formal tests, and it ignores scale reliability. A pair of highly reliable scales should be able to discriminate even when the factor correlation is somewhat high. Pieters et al. (2025) propose the Discriminant Validity Index (DVI), which tests whether a scale’s reliability is high enough, relative to the factor correlation, to support discriminant validity, in two steps.

Step 1, the phi test: is the factor correlation φ meaningfully below 1?

\[\text{DVI}_1 = 1 - |\phi|\]

If DVI₁ is not significantly above zero, stop: discriminant validity has failed.

Step 2, the CR test: even if φ < 1, is the scale reliable enough to “rise above” the factor correlation?

\[\text{DVI}_{CR} = \sqrt{CR} - |\phi|\]

where CR is the congeneric reliability (McDonald’s omega). If DVI_CR > 0 with a confidence interval excluding zero, the scale can distinguish itself from the other construct. For example, with φ = 0.93 and CR = 0.83, √CR ≈ 0.91, which is below 0.93: DVI_CR is negative, and discriminant validity fails. Reliability sets an upper bound on how distinctly a scale can behave; if that bound is below the factor correlation, the scale is compromised.

  EC2  ~~ th_EC2*EC2
  EC3  ~~ th_EC3*EC3
  EC4  ~~ th_EC4*EC4

What to Do When Discriminant Validity Fails

  1. Reconceptualise: are the constructs genuinely different, or two facets of one?
  2. Revise the scale: remove or rewrite items too close in meaning to the other construct, and re-collect.
  3. Combine the scales: if they cannot be separated, treat them as one broader construct (e.g., “pro-environmental orientation”).
  4. Report transparently: at minimum, report the failure and what it implies for interpreting your effects.
ImportantThe core take-home message

An effect on a GPI scale that fails discriminant validity from EC is not clearly interpretable. You cannot claim the campaign increased purchase intention if the scale is indistinguishable from environmental concern. Discriminant validity testing is a prerequisite for meaningful inference about relationships between constructs.

Other Methods for Assessing Discriminant Validity

  • Average Variance Extracted (Fornell & Larcker, 1981): compare the square root of each construct’s AVE with its inter-construct correlations. It was dominant before HTMT but is often too lenient.
  • Multitrait-multimethod analysis (Campbell & Fiske, 1959): measure several traits with several methods and examine the pattern of correlations. Resource-intensive, but the gold standard.
  • Network psychometrics: Gaussian graphical models make discriminant validity failures visible as dense cross-scale connections.

Validity Testing for Non-Likert Data

The CTT equation, observed = true + error, is a property of measurement itself, not of Likert scales. A willingness-to-pay (WTP) response of $20 might reflect true utility of $15 plus $5 of upward error from luxury packaging that inflated perceived market price. A text response of “fine” carries different sentiment depending on who wrote it. In every case the observed signal blends several latent sources:

\[X_\text{observed} = T_\text{true construct} + \lambda \cdot T_\text{other construct} + \varepsilon_\text{random}.\]

The failure mode is identical across formats; what changes is the tools available to diagnose it. HTMT and DVI don’t apply to WTP, choices, or text, but the question they answer, is this measure specific enough to support causal inference?, applies universally. The two worked examples below are in dropdowns.

Willingness to Pay Data

WTP is meant to capture personal utility, but responses also reflect perceived market price. Utility and price belief are conceptually distinct but entangled in one number: \(X_\text{WTP} = T_\text{eco utility} + \lambda \cdot T_\text{perceived price} + \varepsilon\). The worked example gives each participant WTP for six eco-cleaning products, three in plain packaging and three in luxury metallic packaging (a cue that should raise perceived price without changing eco-utility). If WTP for luxury products rises while liking and forced choice barely move, WTP is blending utility with price beliefs.

If the 2-factor model fits substantially better (higher CFI, lower RMSEA and SRMR, significant likelihood-ratio test), plain and luxury WTP are not measuring the same construct. If a green marketing treatment raises WTP, you cannot cleanly claim it raised eco-utility; it may have made the product seem more premium.

Two checks without a full model.

  • Known-groups test: committed environmental activists should show higher WTP for plain-packaged eco products, but similar WTP to non-activists for luxury products if the luxury cue moves price beliefs rather than utility.
  • Experimental falsification: manipulate something that should move price beliefs but not utility (luxury packaging, an inflated “RRP” anchor, a prestige brand cue). If WTP shifts more than liking or forced choice, WTP is contaminated by price beliefs.

The gold standard is a multitrait-multimethod design: eco-utility measured by open-ended WTP, liking ratings, forced choice, an incentivised BDM bid, or observed purchase. Convergent validity holds when the same construct appears across methods; discriminant validity holds when eco-utility measures are more similar to each other than to measures of perceived price or brand prestige.

Textual Data

The word “fine” means different things in a British review, an American survey, or a rushed response: \(X_\text{text} = T_\text{intended construct} + \lambda \cdot T_\text{context / register / fatigue} + \varepsilon\). When you extract a “green purchase intention” signal from open-ended text, it must be specific to purchase intention and not merely general environmental rhetoric. Topic modeling (LDA, STM) is to text what factor analysis is to Likert items, and it can be used to assess whether the constructs it uncovers are specific enough to support causal claims.

When You Have Only One DV: Discriminant Validity as an Identification Problem

Everything so far needed several indicators per construct. Many DVs have one: a single WTP number, a single choice, a single behavior. With one observed variable there is nothing to run HTMT or DVI on, and no statistical test can pull apart two latent sources that enter one number. In econometric terms this is an identification problem: one equation, two unknowns. The data alone cannot say how much of a WTP response is “value” and how much is something else.

That does not remove the researcher’s responsibility to establish discriminant validity. It changes the tool, from a statistical test to an argument from the data-generating process. Use theory to write down what produces the number, name the other latent variables that could move it, and then use the design to supply the independent variation the single measure lacks.

Step 1: Write down the data-generating process

Willingness to pay is typically used as a measure of people’s latent value for a product. But WTP also captures a related, psychologically differentiable construct: people’s price expectations. Evangelidis, Jung, and Moon (2024) review evidence that a hypothetical WTP question leads respondents to imagine themselves as buyers even when they would never buy, and that their answers tend to track the perceived market price of the good: respondents effectively ask “what does this typically cost?” as much as “what is it worth to me?” Vegans, for example, state non-zero WTP for a steak. Writing this as a data-generating process:

\[\text{WTP} = \alpha + \lambda_V \cdot \text{Value} + \lambda_P \cdot \text{PriceExpectation} + \varepsilon .\]

Value and price expectations are related (eco-conscious consumers expect eco products to cost more), which is what makes this a discriminant validity problem and not random noise. Other candidate contaminants include strategic bidding (buyers shading WTP below their valuation to get a good deal), hypothetical bias (overstating when nothing is at stake), and budget constraints. Which of these threaten your inference depends on whether they differ across your conditions or predictors.

Step 2: Ask what independent variation the design can supply

Because the single number cannot separate the sources, the separation has to come from outside it:

Strategy What it does Example with WTP
Measure the rival construct Observe the contaminant directly and test whether your treatment moves it Ask “what would this typically cost?” and compare conditions. Don’t simply control for it: measured after treatment, it is an outcome of treatment
Manipulate the rival construct Move it while leaving the target construct unchanged Vary a reference price or typical-price cue; if WTP moves, price expectations are in the number
Use groups where one component is near zero Any response there must come from the other component Non-dog owners and a dog toy, vegans and a steak: positive WTP reveals the price-expectation component
Change the elicitation Close off the route the contaminant uses An explicit “would not buy” option, or an incentive-compatible procedure such as BDM (which has its own strategic issues)
Triangulate with other measures Use measures with different contaminants Choice, liking ratings, and a consequential valuation; see Module 1, Part 3

Step 3: State what you can and cannot claim

Report the claim the design identifies. “The label raised stated WTP” is supported by randomization. “The label raised consumers’ value for the product” additionally requires evidence that price expectations did not drive the result. If you cannot supply that evidence, scope the claim to the observed DV, say which rival constructs remain open, and say which design feature (a measured expectation item, a price-cue manipulation, an incentive-compatible follow-up) would close them. This is the same logic as Module 1, Part 3, applied to discriminant validity: there, the question was whether the reporting relationship differs across groups; here, it is whether the number measures one construct at all.

Interlude: Reliability Is Not Validity

The Core Distinction

The sections above showed how to detect discriminant validity failures. A related failure trips up researchers constantly: a scale can be highly reliable and still be invalid.

Reliability is consistency: do the items within a scale hang together? Cronbach’s alpha, the most common index, is computed entirely within a scale:

\[\alpha = \frac{k\,\bar{\rho}}{1 + (k-1)\,\bar{\rho}},\]

where \(k\) is the number of items and \(\bar{\rho}\) is the average inter-item correlation within the scale. Nothing in it refers to other constructs, so alpha is blind to discriminant validity by design. A scale can have alpha = 0.95 and an HTMT of 1.20: perfectly reliable at measuring something indistinguishable from another construct.

The Spearman–Brown prophecy makes it worse: adding items always raises alpha, whether or not the new items tap your construct or an adjacent one. Researchers who respond to low alpha by writing more items can push alpha up while discriminant validity erodes. The consequences are inflated correlations between constructs, uninterpretable regression and mediation coefficients, and results that replicate reliably but mean little.
