Part 2: Omitted Variable Bias — A Related Problem in Observational Data

Connecting to Part 1

In Part 1, we asked whether our measure of green purchase intention was actually distinguishing purchase intention from environmental concern. Here we ask a related question at a different level. When we estimate the relationship between advertising and sales, how do we know we’re isolating advertising’s effect rather than picking up other processes that make advertising and sales move together?

The underlying concern is similar. We observe something and want to attribute it to a particular process, but different processes can produce the same observable pattern. Unless we consider those alternatives, we may draw conclusions our data cannot support. The problem is not that outcomes have several causes; it is whether our evidence and assumptions justify attributing an observed quantity or relationship to the process we care about.

The two problems are related, not equivalent. Discriminant validity concerns the interpretation of measures; omitted variable bias (OVB) concerns the interpretation of estimated relationships. Either can exist without the other, and a measurement problem can sometimes produce OVB or make confounding harder to address.

Related problems, but not the same problem
● DV = discriminant validity problem   ● OVB = omitted variable bias

Neither concept is a subset of the other. The overlap means the two problems can coexist, not that measurement contamination always causes OVB when both are present.

The same logic, two equations

Part 1 started from the classical test theory equation and then allowed a second construct to contaminate the observed score. Here is the analogous decomposition for a regression coefficient.

A. Measurement: what is in the observed score?

\[ X_{\text{observed}} = \underbrace{\textcolor{#0072B2}{T_{\text{target}}}}_{\textcolor{#0072B2}{\text{intended construct}}} + \underbrace{\textcolor{#D55E00}{\lambda Z}}_{\textcolor{#D55E00}{\text{other construct}}} + \underbrace{\textcolor{#666666}{\varepsilon}}_{\textcolor{#666666}{\text{random error}}} \]

A purchase-intention score (\(X\)) that reflects purchase intention (\(T\)) and environmental concern (\(Z\)).

B. Estimation: what is in the coefficient?

\[ \beta_{\text{naive}} = \underbrace{\textcolor{#0072B2}{\beta}}_{\textcolor{#0072B2}{\text{target effect}}} + \underbrace{\textcolor{#D55E00}{\gamma\,\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,U)}{\operatorname{Var}(X)}}}_{\textcolor{#D55E00}{\text{omitted-variable contribution}}} \]

An advertising slope (\(\beta_{\text{naive}}\)) that reflects advertising’s effect and store quality (\(U\)).

The parallel: in measurement, the observed score can contain variation associated with another construct; in regression, the observed coefficient can contain an association created by another causal process. But the first equation decomposes an observed score and the second decomposes a regression coefficient. They are analogous, not mathematically interchangeable.

NoteTechnical qualifications
  • Equation A is a stylized measurement model extending the classical test theory starting point; classical test theory itself doesn’t require a contaminating construct. And the simulation in Part 1 generates highly correlated GPI and EC factors; it doesn’t build item-level EC contamination into the GPI items, so treat contamination here as a hypothetical model.
  • In Equation B, \(Y = \alpha + \beta X + \gamma U + \varepsilon\) is a correctly specified additive structural model with \(\varepsilon\) uncorrelated with \(X\) and \(U\). \(\beta_{\text{naive}}\) is the population slope of the regression that omits \(U\), not a finite-sample estimate.

The Scenario

A retail analytics team has secondary data on 500 stores: monthly advertising spend (in standardised units) and monthly sales (a standardised index). The question: does advertising spend predict sales?

What they cannot observe is store quality, a latent construct reflecting location, staff, and store environment. Premium stores advertise more and sell more for reasons unrelated to advertising.

Simulating the Observational Dataset

▶ Simulate observational store dataset (n=500) 
set.seed(2025)
n_obs <- 500

# ── Store quality: continuous, unobserved ─────────────────────────────────────
# Positive = premium, negative = budget. Never in our dataset.
U_quality <- rnorm(n_obs)

# ── Advertising spend: strongly correlated with store quality ──────────────────
# Premium stores invest much more — this is the source of OVB.
X_ads <- 4 * U_quality + rnorm(n_obs, sd = 1.5)

# ── Sales: driven by advertising (small true effect) AND store quality (large) ─
# TRUE advertising coefficient: 0.20 (small). Errors are homoskedastic.
Y_sales <- 0.20 * X_ads + 5.00 * U_quality + rnorm(n_obs, sd = 1.5)

# ── Store size: a strong proxy for quality (corr ≈ 0.95 with U_quality) ──────
store_size <- 1.5 * U_quality + rnorm(n_obs, sd = 0.5)

# ── Observed dataset (quality is hidden; store_size is sometimes available) ───
obs_df <- data.frame(
  advertising = X_ads,
  sales       = Y_sales,
  store_size  = store_size,
  quality     = U_quality    # kept here only because this is a simulation
)

▶ The omitted-variable decomposition for this simulation 
beta_true <- 0.20; gamma_u <- 5.00
var_x     <- 4^2 * 1 + 1.5^2           # Var(X) = 4^2 Var(U) + 1.5^2
cov_xu    <- 4 * 1                      # Cov(X, U) = 4 Var(U)
delta_u   <- cov_xu / var_x             # Cov(X,U)/Var(X)
ovb_term  <- gamma_u * delta_u
beta_naive_pop <- beta_true + ovb_term
b_naive <- unname(coef(m_naive)["advertising"])

The advertising coefficient looks large and highly significant (1.27), but the true coefficient is only 0.20. The omitted-variable equation shows where the rest comes from. Store quality drives sales strongly (\(\gamma = 5\)) and is closely tied to advertising (\(\operatorname{Cov}(X,U)/\operatorname{Var}(X) = 4/18.25 = 0.22\)), so

\[\beta_{\text{naive}} = 0.20 + 5 \times 0.22 = 0.20 + 1.10 \approx 1.30.\]

About 85% of the naive coefficient is variation associated with store quality, not advertising’s direct effect. OLS cannot tell “advertising caused the sales” from “premium stores also advertise more”; the coefficient absorbs both.

When does omitting a variable bias the coefficient?

Having several causes of sales is not enough. In this simple linear model, an omitted variable biases the advertising coefficient only if both hold: it predicts sales after accounting for advertising, and it is associated with advertising.

Associated with advertising Not associated with advertising
Predicts sales OVB (store quality here) No OVB (e.g., an independent store attribute)
Doesn’t predict sales No OVB No OVB

The bias can be positive or negative: its sign is the sign of \(\gamma\) times the sign of \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,U)\).

A residual plot is a good first habit. It can reveal non-linearity, outliers, and non-constant variance. Does it reveal confounding?

A regression can satisfy the usual residual diagnostics and still provide a misleading estimate of the effect we’re interested in. Residual diagnostics help us identify some modeling problems, but they cannot establish that a relationship is unconfounded.

Step 3: Practical Diagnostics With Observational Data

If routine diagnostics can’t detect confounding, what can we do? Three approaches use the same store data. For each, ask what it can and cannot tell us.

A. Think through the confounding structure

Before more statistics, name a plausible common cause of advertising and sales. Here it is store quality, which we can’t observe. We can observe store size, a proxy.

Omitting store quality creates the problem: it affects both advertising and sales. The observed proxy lets us check whether such a characteristic is associated with advertising, and with sales given advertising.

  • What it can tell us: a theoretically relevant pre-existing characteristic is associated with advertising and with sales given advertising, so it is worth treating store quality as a possible confounder.
  • What it can’t: that the proxy is a valid control, or that it captures every unobserved common cause.

B. Coefficient stability across models

Compare three advertising coefficients: the naive model, a model that adds the store-size proxy, and an oracle model that adds true store quality. The oracle is available only because the simulation gives us the data-generating process.

▶ Plot: advertising coefficient across models, with 95% CIs 
m_oracle <- lm(sales ~ advertising + quality, data = obs_df)
cmp <- do.call(rbind, lapply(
  list("Naive\n(advertising only)" = m_naive, "Adds store-size proxy" = m_proxy,
       "Oracle: adds true quality\n(simulation only)" = m_oracle),
  function(m) data.frame(est = coef(m)["advertising"], lo = confint(m)["advertising", 1], hi = confint(m)["advertising", 2])))
cmp$model <- factor(rownames(cmp), levels = rev(rownames(cmp)))

       title = "Accounting for More Relevant Information Moves the Estimate",
       subtitle = "Only the oracle model recovers the true effect") +

The estimate falls from 1.27 (naive) to 0.77 with the proxy, and to 0.29 with the true confounder. Accounting for more relevant information moved us toward the truth.

  • What it can tell us: adding a variable changes the conditional association between advertising and sales. If a theoretically relevant control moves the coefficient, that is a reason to worry about the naive estimate.
  • What it can’t: a coefficient change doesn’t establish OVB, and stability doesn’t rule it out. Whether an adjustment moves us closer to the causal effect depends on the variable’s causal role and how well it is measured. Controlling for a mediator or collider can introduce other problems, so we can’t just add every available predictor.

C. How strong would a hidden confounder have to be?

Rearranging the omitted-variable equation gives a simple sensitivity question: how strongly would an unmeasured confounder \(U\) have to predict sales (\(\gamma\)), and be associated with advertising (\(\delta = \operatorname{Cov}(X,U)/\operatorname{Var}(X)\), the slope of \(U\) on advertising), to account for the coefficient we observed? If \(U\) is measured in SD units, the adjusted advertising effect is the observed coefficient minus \(\gamma\,\delta\).

▶ Heatmap: advertising effect implied by hypothetical confounder strengths 
grid <- expand.grid(gamma = seq(0, 6, by = 0.1), delta = seq(0, 0.4, by = 0.005))
grid$adjusted <- b_naive - grid$gamma * grid$delta         # observed coefficient minus the omitted-variable term

# Observed benchmark: what the store-size proxy alone accounts for (standardised, so units match)
z_size  <- as.numeric(scale(obs_df$store_size))
g_bench <- unname(coef(lm(sales ~ advertising + z_size, data = obs_df))["z_size"])
d_bench <- unname(coef(lm(z_size ~ advertising, data = obs_df))["advertising"])
stopifnot(abs((b_naive - coef(m_proxy)["advertising"]) - g_bench * d_bench) < 1e-8)   # OLS identity

pts <- data.frame(gamma = c(gamma_u, g_bench), delta = c(delta_u, d_bench),
                  label = c("True confounder\n(simulation only)", "Store size\n(observed benchmark)"))
ggplot(grid, aes(delta, gamma, fill = adjusted)) +
  geom_raster() +
  geom_contour(aes(z = adjusted), breaks = 0, colour = "black", linewidth = 0.8) +

                       name = "Adjusted\nadvertising\neffect") +

  geom_text(data = pts, aes(delta, gamma, label = label), inherit.aes = FALSE, hjust = c(1.12, -0.12), vjust = 0.5, size = 3.3) +
  annotate("text", x = 0.025, y = 5.7, hjust = 0, size = 3.3, label = "black line: confounder would\nexplain the whole coefficient") +
  labs(x = expression("Association with advertising (" * delta * ", SD of U per unit advertising)"),
       y = expression("Effect on sales (" * gamma * ", per SD of U)"),
       title = "Sensitivity to an Omitted Confounder",
       subtitle = "Adjusted effect = observed coefficient − γ × δ") +
  theme_minimal(base_size = 12) + theme(panel.grid = element_blank())

Anything on or beyond the black line would fully account for the coefficient. The gold marker for the true confounder sits where the adjusted effect is about 0.2; the store-size benchmark shows how much an observed covariate of that strength accounts for.

  • What it can tell us: how strong an unmeasured confounder would need to be to explain the result, and whether that seems plausible given what we know (here we can calibrate against an observed covariate).
  • What it can’t: it isn’t a test, and it doesn’t identify the actual omitted confounder. In real research the plausible ranges need substantive justification.

No combination of routine regression diagnostics can prove that unobserved confounding is absent.

When the Control Is Itself a Measure

We used store size as a proxy for store quality, but store size is not store quality. This brings back the measurement question from Part 1: what happens when the control is an imperfect measure of the confounder? The proxy reduced the bias (from 1.27 to 0.77) without eliminating it, even though it correlates 0.95 with true quality: the part of quality it misses still operates through the large \(\gamma\). A reliable proxy can also have construct-validity problems (store size may reflect real-estate costs more than quality), and reliability and discriminant validity are distinct issues. So the validity of our controls matters as much as the validity of our outcomes. Module 3, Part 2 treats how poorly measured covariates undermine conditional independence.

Summary: Three Distinctions

Related questions, but each needs different evidence:

  • Measurement: are we measuring the construct we think we’re measuring? (Discriminant validity; Part 1.)
  • Estimation: does our coefficient isolate the relationship or effect we intend to estimate? (OVB; this part.)
  • Identification: what features of the data-generating process and research design justify that interpretation?

Looking ahead to Module 2: randomization helps separate treatment assignment from pre-existing confounders, in expectation. It cannot automatically establish the construct validity of the outcome.

The residual patterns that do appear in real data may also point to latent subgroups (Part 4) or collective outliers (Part 5), which are different problems from confounding.

Other Methods for Diagnosing Endogeneity and OVB

The diagnostics covered here are entry-level approaches. For more rigorous identification:

NoteComing up in Module 3

Several of the methods below are covered in depth in Module 3: Causal Inference. Regression discontinuity design (Module 3, Part 3), difference-in-differences (Module 3, Part 3), and propensity score / matching methods (Module 3, Part 2) each get their own tutorial section with worked examples.

  • Instrumental variables (IV) / Two-Stage Least Squares (2SLS): Find a variable that predicts X but does not directly affect Y (an “instrument”) and use it to isolate the exogenous variation in X. Powerful, but requires strong theoretical justification.
  • Regression discontinuity design (RDD): If assignment is based on a threshold cutoff, the discontinuity creates locally exogenous variation. Covered in Module 3, Part 3.
  • Difference-in-differences (DiD): Compare changes over time in treated vs. control units. Requires parallel trends. Covered in Module 3, Part 3.
  • Propensity score methods: Model the probability of “treatment” given observed covariates, then match or weight. Handles observed confounders only. Covered in Module 3, Part 2.
  • Hausman test: Tests whether OLS and IV estimates differ significantly. Requires a valid instrument.
  • Sensitivity analysis (Oster, 2019): How large would the omitted variable’s effect need to be, relative to included controls, to explain away the estimated coefficient? Diagnostic C above is a simplified version of this logic.
  • Panel data fixed effects: With repeated observations per unit, within-unit fixed effects remove all time-invariant confounders, including unobserved store quality.
